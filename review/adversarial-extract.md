# Extract for external review
Thirty-four records from a longitudinal research corpus about Indian government policy, plus three subjects the project examined and decided **not** to record. Presented for adversarial review: the question is what a reader objects to, in the reasoning, the sourcing, the framing, or the decision to record at all.
## How to read this, and what has been done to it
This text has been **processed**, and a reviewer should discount accordingly. Record identifiers, cross-references between records, internal category names and rule numbers have been removed or replaced with neutral phrases, so that the framework the project uses is not visible and cannot be deferred to. Where a record referred to another by identifier, the reference now reads "another record in this corpus" — the comparison was real, its target is not shown.

Scores are rendered as sentences rather than as the vocabulary the project files them under. The project’s own rulebook is deliberately **not** included: the point is what you object to without knowing what it considers acceptable.

**Source tiers are kept**, because sourcing cannot be judged without them. They run T1 to T5: T1 a primary document from the body responsible, T2 an international or statutory body’s own publication, T3 a specialist or academic secondary source, T4 press or advocacy reporting relayed rather than retrieved, T5 an index or composite score. A record’s claims are expected to rest no higher than its best source supports.

**Caveats are kept in full.** They are where this corpus does most of its hedging, and a review that never saw them would be attacking something weaker than the real thing.
## Method, offered for attack
The corpus records discrete government actions and asks, of each, what was claimed at the outset and what can be established since. Four commitments are worth stating because they are the method’s load-bearing choices, and each can be argued with:

1. **A source that could not be retrieved is not cited as though it had been.** Where a document was described from a secondary account, the record says so and is tiered down.
2. **Where two instruments measure the same quantity and disagree, neither figure is adopted and no midpoint is taken.** The disagreement is recorded as the finding.
3. **An absence is recorded only when the place it would appear was checked.** The record states which document was searched and what else that document does contain.
4. **Every record carries a case for and a case against**, and the project holds that a record presenting only one is defective regardless of which side it favours.

Objections to any of these are more useful than objections to individual figures.

### 1. The 2016 Rafale agreement bought 36 aircraft off the shelf and dropped 108 that were to be built in India, and the item-wise price is withheld under a 2008 bilateral agreement
*Dated 2016-09-23.*

**What this is.** An Inter-Governmental Agreement was signed on 23 September 2016 with France for 36 Rafale aircraft in direct fly-away condition, including weapons, long-term maintenance support, simulators, ten years of annual maintenance and associated equipment. It replaced the Medium Multi Role Combat Aircraft proposal for 126 aircraft, of which 18 were to be direct fly-away and 108 licence-manufactured in India. The fly-away component doubled from 18 to 36; the licence-manufactured component went from 108 to none. The Ministry has declined to publish item-wise cost, citing the confidentiality provisions of a 2008 India-France security agreement, and states that an approximate acquisition cost was given to Parliament.

**What was claimed at the outset.** The Ministry's own comparison against the MMRCA proposal, in a Rajya Sabha written reply: "In the IGA, better terms have been achieved in terms of better pricing, better maintenance terms and better delivery schedule." Restated in 2018 as a deal "better in terms of capability, price, equipment, delivery, maintenance, training".

**What can be established since.** The structural change is the one the announcement does not lead with. MMRCA was 18 fly-away plus 108 built in India under licence; the IGA is 36 fly-away and nothing built in India. Measured on aircraft procured the programme shrank by 90; measured on aircraft to be manufactured domestically it went to zero. The Ministry's stated grounds for the change concern price, maintenance and delivery — the three axes on which it claims improvement — and the domestic-manufacture element is not among the terms it compares.

**The case for.** MMRCA had already failed as a process — the Ministry's account is that the 126-aircraft procurement "ran aground" and that a personal ministerial veto in 2012 stopped the institutional process while the Air Force's fighter strength was declining. A licence-manufacture commitment that never produces an aircraft delivers no capability and no industry, and 36 in fly-away condition delivered aircraft on a stated schedule where the alternative had delivered none in fourteen years. The fly-away component actually doubled. A government-to-government agreement also carries state-level obligations that a vendor contract does not, which matters over a ten-year maintenance term.

**The case against.** The industrial content was the part that could not be recovered later, and it was the part dropped. 108 aircraft built in India was the largest transfer-of-manufacture commitment in the pipeline, and nothing in the IGA replaces it — the record shows no licence production, no production line and no equivalent obligation. Against the Aatmanirbharta framing that governs every other record in this arc, the flagship fighter acquisition of the period moved procurement in the opposite direction. And the comparison the Ministry offers cannot be checked: it claims better pricing while declining to publish the price, so the central term of its own defence of the decision is the one term no reader can test.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Contested, and the reason is that the two sides are arguing about different objectives rather than about the same one. Read as a fighter-acquisition decision the claim is testable in principle on price, maintenance and delivery, and the Ministry asserts improvement on all three. Read as an industrial decision it removed the largest single licence-manufacture commitment in the Indian defence pipeline. Both readings use the same facts, and the record does not choose because the announcement itself does not say which objective governed.

**On the competing accounts.** No competing accounts of any fact here — the aircraft counts, the dates and the structure are stated once by the Ministry and not disputed in anything retrieved. The disagreement is about which objective the decision should be judged against, which is a difference of frame rather than of fact.

**Not measured.** The item-wise cost of the 2016 Rafale contract — The Ministry refused it in terms, to an identifiable demand, on 7 February 2018: "The demand that the Government disclose the details and value of the contract for the Rafale aircraft contracted in 2016 is unrealistic", on the ground that item-wise cost would reveal customisations and weapons configurations and "would also come under the ambit of the security agreement signed in 2008". A named refusal to a specific demand, with a stated legal ground, is what distinguishes withheld from held but unreleased. The same statement says an approximate acquisition cost was provided to Parliament; that figure was not retrieved in this run and no price is stated in this record. The stated reason is that release was specifically requested and refused. What would settle it: The Parliamentary answer carrying the approximate acquisition cost, or the Comptroller and Auditor General's report on the acquisition, which was tabled and would carry the audited comparison.

**Not measured.** Whether any domestic-manufacture obligation replaced the 108 licence-built aircraft — The IGA is described as covering weapons, maintenance, simulators and associated equipment, and nothing retrieved states an offset or transfer-of-manufacture obligation that substitutes for the licence production the MMRCA proposal carried. Absence of a statement is not a statement of absence, and the record says only that none was located. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: The offset schedule for the 2016 IGA, or a Ministry statement of offset obligations discharged against it.

**Caveat.** No price figure appears in this record. The Ministry states that an approximate acquisition cost was given to Parliament, so a public figure exists; it was not retrieved here and none is quoted, inferred or reconstructed. Nothing in this record asserts that the contract value is unknown to Parliament — only that it was not retrieved in this run and that item-wise cost is withheld.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* Ministry of Defence, "Agreement with France for supply of Rafale Fighter Jets", PIB, 18 December 2017 — Rajya Sabha written reply of Raksha Mantri Nirmala Sitharaman: the 23 September 2016 IGA for 36 aircraft, the MMRCA structure of 18 fly-away plus 108 licence-manufactured, and the better-pricing/maintenance/delivery claim
- *(T1)* Ministry of Defence, "Information Relating to Inter-Governmental Agreement on Rafale", PIB, 7 February 2018 — the refusal to disclose item-wise cost, the 2008 India-France security agreement as its ground, and the statement that an approximate acquisition cost was provided to Parliament

### 2. The debt-restructuring instrument was named in December 2024 and existed by April 2025, and the assistance around it is stated in three currencies, two of them without a rate, and never totalled
*Dated 2025-04-05.*

**What this is.** At the India-Sri Lanka summit of 16 December 2024 the two leaders "instructed officials to finalize discussions on the bilateral MoU on Debt Restructuring". By the joint press statement of 5 April 2025 in Colombo the Prime Minister referred to "our bilateral 'Debt Restructuring Agreement'" as an existing instrument, and announced that in the preceding six months India had converted loans worth more than US$100 million into grants, that interest rates had been reduced that day, and that a support package of approximately 2.4 billion Lankan Rupees would be provided for the Eastern Provinces. The December statement records India as co-chair of the Official Creditors' Committee, emergency financing and forex support worth US$4 billion, and US$20.66 million to settle payments due for projects completed under existing Lines of Credit.

**What was claimed at the outset.** Two things, and only the first is a commitment. The commitment: to finalise discussions on the bilateral MoU on Debt Restructuring, stated on 16 December 2024 with no date attached. The framing: a "strategic shift from debt-driven models towards investment led partnerships", which the two leaders agreed "would ensure a more sustainable path to economic recovery" — an assertion about a direction, carrying no target.

**What can be established since.** RESOLVED, AND QUICKLY. The instrument named on 16 December 2024 as something officials were instructed to finalise is spoken of on 5 April 2025 as existing — a bilateral Debt Restructuring Agreement, referred to in the Prime Minister's own voice — which is delivery inside four months against a commitment that carried no deadline at all. THE QUANTITIES AROUND IT DO NOT COMBINE. Four figures are given across the two documents: US$4 billion of "emergency financing and forex support", US$20.66 million to settle payments due under existing Lines of Credit, "more than US$100 million" of loans converted into grants, and approximately 2.4 billion Lankan Rupees for the Eastern Provinces. They are in two currencies with no rate published, they mix a facility aggregate with specific transactions, one of them is a floor rather than a figure, and no document retrieved states a total of relief extended or debt reduced.

**The case for.** This is a commitment kept faster than it was required to be, on a matter where slowness is the norm. The December statement set no date; four months later the instrument existed and interest rates had been cut on top of it. India co-chaired the Official Creditors' Committee — a role, not a cheque, and the harder of the two things to do — and the Sri Lankan President's own account credits that co-chairing as instrumental in finalising the wider restructuring in time. Converting Lines of Credit into grants is the form of relief that actually removes debt rather than deferring it, and India did it repeatedly: US$20.66 million to clear payments due on completed projects, then more than US$100 million of loans converted over six months. The April statement also puts these figures in India's own voice, which the December one did not.

**The case against.** Nothing here can be added up, and the presentation makes that harder rather than easier. US$4 billion covers "emergency financing and forex support" — a disbursement and a facility, which are different quantities — with no breakdown, no period and no statement of how much was drawn or repaid, so the headline figure describes neither a stock nor a flow. "More than US$100 million" is a floor and not a measurement. And the Eastern Provinces package is stated in Lankan Rupees while everything around it is in dollars, with no rate given, which leaves the smallest headline commitment in the speech carrying its largest digits — 2.4 billion beside 20.66 million. In the December statement every figure for Indian assistance sits in the Sri Lankan President's mouth, thanked for rather than asserted; the Prime Minister's own paragraph contains no number at all.

**Verdict.** The project judges this a split: part of it delivered, part cannot be scored. The delivery half worked and the measurement half does not permit scoring. A named instrument moved from instruction to existence in four months, which is faster than most of this stage of the work commitments and is scored as such. What cannot be scored is the assistance: the figures are not commensurable, no aggregate is published, and the largest of them has no breakdown, so whether the relief was large or small against Sri Lanka's obligations is not answerable from anything retrieved. Partly, on that split — not as a hedge between the two.

**On the competing accounts.** No competing accounts. The US$20.66 million of December 2024 and the "more than US$100 million" of April 2025 are not two versions of one figure and are not filed as such: the first settles payments due on completed Line of Credit projects, the second converts loans to grants over a later six-month window. Different acts, possibly overlapping, and nothing retrieved states the relationship between them — which is recorded as unmeasured rather than resolved by assumption.

**Not measured.** The composition of the US$4 billion — how much was emergency financing, how much forex support, over what period, and how much has been drawn or repaid — The figure appears once, as a round aggregate, in the Sri Lankan President's expression of thanks. No Indian government document retrieved breaks it down, dates it, or distinguishes facility from disbursement. As with the Maldives swap in another record in this corpus, a facility and a drawing are different quantities and the published number does not say which it is. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: A Ministry of Finance or Department of Economic Affairs statement of assistance to Sri Lanka by instrument and year, or a Parliamentary answer giving the breakdown.

**Not measured.** The total value of debt relief extended to Sri Lanka, and the exchange rate applied to the 2.4 billion Lankan Rupee package — Four figures are published across the two statements in two currencies, and no document retrieved totals them or gives a rate at which the Rupee figure could be placed beside the dollar ones. The rate exists and is not stated; the total is a sum the publishers have not performed. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: An Indian government statement of cumulative assistance to Sri Lanka in one currency, or the Eastern Provinces package restated in dollars.

**Not measured.** The text, date of signature and terms of the bilateral Debt Restructuring Agreement — It is referred to as existing on 5 April 2025 and was still being finalised on 16 December 2024, so it was concluded between those dates. Neither the date nor any term of it appears in anything retrieved, and the April reference is a sentence in a press statement rather than an announcement of the instrument. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: The text of the Agreement, or a Ministry of Finance announcement of its signature and principal terms.

**Caveat.** NO CURRENCY CONVERSION IS PERFORMED HERE. The 2.4 billion Lankan Rupee figure is not restated in dollars and is not added to any dollar figure, because no rate appears in the source and applying an external one would attach a precision to the comparison that the publisher did not offer. For the same reason no total of assistance is stated. The 5 April 2025 document is the Prime Minister's remarks at a joint press statement — a speech, not an agreement text — and this record treats its figures as announced rather than as audited. The grid interconnection is noted here only as it was framed on that occasion, as creating opportunities for Sri Lanka to export electricity; the link itself is the single India-Sri Lanka row in the Ministry of Power table another record in this corpus, where it is also the one entry for which no operating history can exist.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* Prime Minister's Office, "India - Sri Lanka Joint Statement: Fostering Partnerships for a Shared Future", PIB, 16 December 2024 — paragraph 10: emergency financing and forex support worth US$4 billion, India as co-chair of the Official Creditors' Committee, US$20.66 million to settle payments due for projects completed under existing Lines of Credit, and the instruction to officials to finalize discussions on the bilateral MoU on Debt Restructuring; paragraph 7 on converting Line of Credit projects to grant assistance; paragraph 11 on the shift from debt-driven models to investment-led partnerships; paragraph 9 on training 1,500 Sri Lankan civil servants over five years
- *(T1)* Prime Minister's Office, "Prime Minister's remarks during Joint Press Statement with the President of Sri Lanka", PIB, 5 April 2025 — "in the last 6 months alone, we have converted loans worth more than USD 100 million into grants", "our bilateral 'Debt Restructuring Agreement'" as an existing instrument, the decision to reduce interest rates, the approximately 2.4 billion Lankan Rupee support package for the Eastern Provinces, and the Grid Inter-Connectivity Agreement framed as creating opportunities for Sri Lanka to export electricity

### 3. India has not been sanctioned under CAATSA for the S-400, and that is not the same as having been waived — the statute distinguishes three states and the public record separates only one
*Dated 2017-08-02.*

**What this is.** Section 231(a) of the Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act provides that the President "shall impose five or more of the sanctions described in section 235" on a person determined to have knowingly engaged in a significant transaction with the Russian defence or intelligence sectors. India contracted for the S-400 in 2018. No sanction has been imposed. The statute makes three different things capable of producing that outcome, each with its own instrument: no determination under (a); a waiver under (b); or a delay under (c). Only the first would necessarily be visible from outside.

**What can be established since.** The three states, as the statute defines them. (a) NO DETERMINATION: the duty to sanction arises only once the President determines a significant transaction occurred, and nothing compels that determination to be made or announced. (b) WAIVER — and its conditions are the striking part: a waiver requires the President to submit to congressional committees both a written determination that it is in the vital national security interests of the United States or will further enforcement, AND "a certification that the Government of the Russian Federation has made significant efforts to reduce the number and intensity of cyber intrusions conducted by that Government". That second condition is a finding about RUSSIA, not about India, so a waiver for India turns on a judgement India cannot influence. (c) DELAY: available if the President certifies to the committees, not less frequently than every 180 days, that the person is substantially reducing its significant transactions.

**The case for.** Nothing has happened to India under this statute in eight years, through two administrations and after the S-400 deliveries began, and at some point a consistent non-event is evidence about intent even without an instrument. The strategic reasoning is not hidden either: sanctioning India would be an unusual way to pursue an Indo-Pacific partnership. A reader who wants to know whether India faces CAATSA exposure in practice has a reasonable answer, which is that it has not materialised.

**The case against.** An unexercised discretion is not a waiver and does not have a waiver's properties. A waiver under (b) is an instrument with a date, conditions and a submission to Congress; no determination under (a) is simply a decision not yet taken, and it can be taken later on the same facts, because the transaction does not expire. Collapsing the two turns a standing exposure into a settled question. The statutory language is mandatory once the determination is made — "shall impose five or more" — so the whole of India's protection lies in a determination not being made, which nothing published constrains.

**Verdict.** The project judges that no target was announced against which this could have succeeded or failed. Not an Indian measure and not scorable as one — CAATSA is a United States statute with a United States objective. Recorded because the sentence "India got a CAATSA waiver" is common and, on everything retrieved here, unsupported: no waiver instrument has been located, and the absence of sanctions is not one.

**On the competing accounts.** No competing accounts of any fact. The disagreement in commentary is about what the absence of a sanction MEANS, and both readings accept that no sanction has been imposed and that no waiver instrument is public.

**Not measured.** Whether a section 231(b) waiver or a section 231(c) delay has been issued in respect of India — Both are submissions to congressional committees rather than public actions, so their absence from the public record does not establish that they were not made. The absence CAN be established for the sanctions action itself: a determination under (a) followed by sanctions produces a published Federal Register notice, as the Department of State's notice of 7 April 2021 shows for Turkey's Presidency of Defense Industries, and a search of the Federal Register for CAATSA documents naming India returns nothing of that kind. The positive control is that same Turkey notice — the mechanism does produce a published instrument when it is used, so its absence for India is informative about sanctions and silent about waivers. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: A congressional committee record of a submission under section 231(b) or (c) naming India, or a Presidential determination published under Executive Order 13849.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* Countering America's Adversaries Through Sanctions Act, Public Law 115-44, section 231 (22 U.S.C. 9525) — subsection (a) mandatory imposition, (b) waiver authority and its Russian-cyber certification condition, (c) delay authority and its 180-day certification
- *(T1)* Department of State, Notice of Department of State Sanctions Actions, 86 FR 18017, 7 April 2021 — the section 231(a) determination and sanctions against Turkey's Presidency of Defense Industries, effective 14 December 2020; cited as the positive control that a determination produces a published instrument

### 4. The Indus Waters Treaty was put in abeyance on 23 April 2025, and the word abeyance appears nowhere in the treaty, whose only exit is a duly ratified treaty concluded by both Governments
*Dated 2025-04-23.*

**What this is.** Following the Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025, in which 26 people were killed, the Cabinet Committee on Security decided that the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 "will be held in abeyance with immediate effect, until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism". The same decision closed the Integrated Check Post at Attari, suspended bilateral trade, and excluded Pakistani nationals from the SAARC Visa Exemption Scheme. The treaty's own final provisions, Article XII, provide two mechanisms and no others: modification "by a duly ratified treaty concluded for that purpose between the two Governments", and continuation in force "until terminated by a duly ratified treaty concluded for that purpose between the two Governments".

**What was claimed at the outset.** A stated condition rather than a stated outcome: the treaty is held in abeyance until Pakistan "credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism". The Press Information Bureau's account frames the measure as severing an economic lifeline without escalating direct military conflict, and quotes the formulation "Blood and water cannot flow together".

**What can be established since.** THE TREATY CONTAINS NO MECHANISM ANSWERING TO WHAT WAS DONE, AND THIS IS CHECKABLE RATHER THAN ARGUABLE. Across the full text and all annexures — 215,794 characters — the words abeyance, suspension, termination and denounce do not appear at all. The word terminated appears once, in Article XII(4), and requires a duly ratified treaty concluded by both Governments. The two occurrences of suspend are the arbitral Court suspending its own proceedings pending replacement of an arbitrator under Annexure G, and the three occurrences of withdraw are withdrawals of water from named tributaries., WITH A TRIGGER THAT NAMES NO TEST. The condition for reversal is an assessment of another state's conduct — credibly and irrevocably abjuring support for cross-border terrorism — and nothing published states who judges it, against what criterion, or on what evidence.

**The case for.** A state that believes it is being attacked from across a border is not obliged to keep every instrument of cooperation running unchanged, and the treaty is among the very few points of leverage India holds that does not require force. The measure was calibrated to that: the Press Information Bureau's account presents it explicitly as pressure applied without escalating direct military conflict, which is the choice most likely to avoid deaths. The condition attached is a condition for RESTORATION, not a demand for territory or payment — the treaty returns if the conduct stops — and stating a reversal condition at all is more than a purely punitive measure would have offered. The treaty has also survived three wars and been administered continuously since 1960, which is the strongest argument that India has treated it as durable rather than disposable, and makes the departure a considered one rather than a reflex.

**The case against.** The instrument's own terms provide exactly two routes, modification and termination, and both require the agreement of both Governments; the treaty offers no unilateral exit and no intermediate state, and a checked search of its full text finds no vocabulary for one. The government's own account does not settle which act it performed: one sentence describes "India's termination of the Indus Waters Treaty" and the next says the treaty "will be held in abeyance", and termination and abeyance are not the same legal act. The treaty is also expressly limited by Article XI(1)(a) to rights and obligations concerning the use of the waters and matters incidental thereto, while the condition attached to its abeyance concerns conduct outside that subject entirely. And a reversal condition assessed by one party alone, against criteria it has not published, is indistinguishable in operation from an indefinite one.

**Verdict.** The project judges this a split: part of it delivered, part cannot be scored. The action itself is established and in effect, and its instrument-side facts are now fully checkable. What cannot be scored is whether it achieves its stated end, because the end names no evaluator and no test: a condition that only one party assesses, by criteria it has not published, cannot be shown to be met or unmet at any future date. This record does NOT adjudicate the treaty-law question. Whether abeyance is available to a party outside the treaty's own terms is a question for bodies whose material could not be retrieved here, and the record confines itself to what the treaty says and what the decision says.

**Not measured.** India's stated legal basis for placing the treaty in abeyance — The measure was announced as a decision. Nothing retrieved states the provision, doctrine or instrument relied upon, and the treaty's own text supplies no mechanism of that name. This is recorded as absent from what was retrieved, not as non-existent — a legal position may have been stated in material this run could not reach, and the Permanent Court of Arbitration's site refused two independent clients with HTTP 403. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: A Ministry of External Affairs or Ministry of Law statement of the legal basis, a Parliamentary answer, or India's filings in any proceeding on the question.

**Not measured.** Who determines whether Pakistan has "credibly and irrevocably" abjured support for cross-border terrorism, and by what test — The condition is the sole stated route back to the treaty's operation, and no evaluator, criterion, evidentiary standard or review point appears in anything retrieved. Without one the condition cannot be shown to be satisfied, which means the abeyance cannot be shown to have ended or to have failed to end. The stated reason is that no agreed definition of the quantity exists, so it could not be collected even in principle. What would settle it: Any published criterion, review mechanism or periodic assessment attached to the decision.

**Not measured.** What has changed in the actual operation of the treaty since 23 April 2025 — data sharing, Permanent Indus Commission meetings, or flows — Abeyance is a status; what it altered in practice is a separate question. Nothing retrieved states whether Commission meetings ceased, whether hydrological data transmission stopped, or whether any physical flow changed. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: A Ministry of Jal Shakti or Permanent Indus Commission statement, or a Parliamentary answer on the treaty's operation since the decision.

**Caveat.** The zero counts above are from the treaty's own full text as registered with the United Nations, which is the natural place for its exit provisions and covers the whole instrument; they are a checked absence, not a failed search. THE NON-ZERO COUNTS WERE READ BEFORE THEY WERE USED, and this matters: `suspend` returns two hits and `withdraw` three, and banked as counts they would have supported the opposite conclusion — both `suspend` hits are the arbitral Court pausing its own proceedings, and all three `withdraw` hits are withdrawals of water. The figures the Press Information Bureau gives for Pakistan's dependence on the Indus system — 80 per cent of 16 million hectares of agricultural land, 93 per cent of total water use, 237 million people, one-fourth of GDP — are reproduced here as that document's claims about another country, with no basis, method or source stated in it, and are not adopted by this record. The Permanent Court of Arbitration proceedings are not covered: `pca-cpa.org` and `pcacases.com` both returned HTTP 403 to independent requests, so nothing about them is asserted either way.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* United Nations Treaty Series, Volume 419, No. 6032 — The Indus Waters Treaty 1960 (with annexes), signed at Karachi on 19 September 1960 by India, Pakistan and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development, registered by India on 16 January 1962; Article XII(3) and XII(4) on modification and termination by duly ratified treaty, and Article XI(1)(a) limiting the Treaty to the use of the waters and matters incidental thereto. Full text with a machine-readable layer, 215,794 characters, scanned in full through the corpus-search helper
- *(T1)* Press Information Bureau, "Operation SINDOOR: India's Strategic Clarity and Calculated Force", Backgrounder, 12 May 2025 — the Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025 and its 26 deaths; the Indus Waters Treaty of 1960 to be "held in abeyance with immediate effect, until Pakistan credibly and irrevocably abjures its support for cross-border terrorism", described in the preceding sentence as "India's termination of the Indus Waters Treaty"; the closure of the Integrated Check Post Attari with return by 1 May 2025; the suspension of all bilateral trade; and the exclusion of Pakistani nationals from SAARC Visa Exemption Scheme visas

### 5. The India-UK trade agreement is in force, and the two headline concessions are not the same measurement: 99 per cent of India's exports by value against 24.5 per cent of Britain's on day one
*Dated 2026-07-15.*

**What this is.** The India-UK Comprehensive Economic and Trade Agreement entered into force on 15 July 2026, alongside a Double Contribution Convention on social security whose exemption period was raised from three years to five. CETA was concluded on 6 May 2025 after fourteen negotiating rounds and signed in London on 24 July 2025. The UK gives zero-duty access to nearly 99 per cent of India's exports, covering almost 100 per cent of the trade value. India offers concessions on 89.5 per cent of its tariff lines, covering 91 per cent of UK exports — but only 24.5 per cent of UK export VALUE receives immediate duty-free access, the rest being phased.

**What was claimed at the outset.** The India-UK Roadmap 2030, adopted in May 2021 alongside the Enhanced Trade Partnership, set the goal of doubling bilateral trade to US$100bn by 2030. CETA is presented as the instrument, and as balancing liberalisation against the protection of sensitive sectors through exclusions and phased reductions.

**What can be established since.** NOT YET DUE, TRIGGER NAMED: 2030, for the US$100bn doubling. The agreement is three weeks old at the date of this record and nothing about the trade outcome is testable. What is recorded is the structure, and one feature of it that the published framing does not draw out. THE TWO CONCESSIONS ARE MEASURED DIFFERENTLY AND ARE ROUTINELY QUOTED SIDE BY SIDE. Britain's is stated as a share of India's exports AND of trade value, at zero duty, with no phasing mentioned: 99 per cent of exports, almost 100 per cent of value. India's headline is a share of TARIFF LINES — 89.5 per cent — and the value figure attached to it is 24.5 per cent immediate. A line count and a value share are different quantities, and an immediate concession and a phased one are different commitments; 99 against 89.5 compares neither like with like.

**The case for.** The asymmetry runs in India's favour and that is the plain reading of the numbers. India's exports get near-total immediate zero-duty access to a high-income market; Britain's get a line-count concession most of whose value arrives later. For a country whose standing complaint about trade agreements is that they open its market faster than they open others', this one is drawn the other way, and the phasing is the mechanism that does it. The services and mobility side is substantive rather than decorative — the Double Contribution Convention's exemption period went from three years to five, which is a direct cost saving for Indian professionals posted to the UK, and the Ministry describes the UK's services commitments as among the most ambitious it has offered in any FTA. Fourteen rounds and a fourteen-month gap between signature and entry into force also indicate ratification was done properly rather than announced early.

### 6. Three quantities are called indigenisation: one has a defined formula, one is a production total, and the headline share has no stated basis at all
*Dated 2020-09-28.*

**What this is.** Defence Acquisition Procedure 2020 raised the minimum indigenous content required in each acquisition category and — this is the part usually left out — fixed how it is measured: "IC will now be calculated on 'Base Contract Price' i.e. Total Contract Price less taxes & duties". The minimums are Buy (Indian-IDDM) 50 per cent, up from 40; Buy (Indian) 50 per cent where the design is indigenous and 60 otherwise, up from 40; Buy & Make (Indian) 50 per cent of the Make portion; Buy (Global – Manufacture in India) 50 per cent of Buy plus Make; Buy (Global) 30 per cent for Indian vendors. Separately, the Ministry reports indigenous defence production rising from ₹46,429 crore in FY2014-15 to ₹1.78 lakh crore in FY2025-26, and states that "nearly 65% of defence equipment is now produced domestically, compared to import dependence of 65–70% earlier".

**What was claimed at the outset.** That raising the indigenous-content minimums across categories, with a verification process and a stated calculation basis, would shift procurement toward domestic design and manufacture.

**What can be established since.** The basis for the CONTRACTUAL measure is defined and published, and this record says so plainly because a defined basis is worth recording as clearly as a missing one. The two figures usually quoted alongside it are different quantities. Indigenous production is a value aggregate, not a contractual share, and nothing in it is computed on Base Contract Price. The "nearly 65 per cent" is a share of "defence equipment", and no basis is stated for it anywhere retrieved here: not whether it is by value or by count, not whether the population is annual procurement or the standing inventory, and not what date "earlier" refers to in the 65-70 per cent it is compared against.

**The case for.** The government did the hard part and did it explicitly. An indigenous-content rule is worthless without a formula, because every vendor would compute it differently and no bid would be comparable; DAP 2020 states the denominator, excludes taxes and duties so tax changes cannot move the number, and instituted a verification process. The category minimums rose by ten points across the board on a published table with the old and new figures side by side, which is an auditable commitment rather than a slogan. And the direction is not in doubt: production up from ₹46,429 crore to roughly 3.8 times that in eleven years is a real change in domestic output whatever share it represents.

**The case against.** The number that governs procurement and the number that is published are not the same number, and only the second is ever quoted. "Nearly 65 per cent of defence equipment produced domestically" is the sentence that circulates, and it has no stated denominator, no stated population and no date for the baseline it improves on — so it cannot be checked, cannot be reproduced, and cannot be compared with the DAP figure it is taken to vindicate. A production total rising is consistent with the indigenous share falling if procurement rose faster, and nothing published lets a reader rule that out. The reform defined a metric and then reported against a different one.

**Verdict.** The project judges this a split: part of it delivered, part cannot be scored. Partly, and the split is between the two halves of the same reform. The measurement half worked: DAP 2020 set category minimums and fixed the calculation basis, and both are published and checkable. The reporting half did not follow: the figures the Ministry leads with are a production total and an unattributed equipment share, neither computed on the basis DAP defines, so the reform's own metric is not the one the public sees. Scored against the objective stated at announcement — a shift toward domestic design and manufacture, measured by indigenous content — which is testable in principle precisely because DAP defined it.

**On the competing accounts.** Not a dispute: one government publishes all three figures and no one contradicts them. The relation between them is the incommensurability case — a per-contract contractual floor, a production aggregate and an equipment share are three different quantities, and the record's point is that they are read as one. NOTHING HERE IS PLACED SIDE BY SIDE AS CORROBORATION: that the production total rose while the DAP minimums rose is not evidence that the minimums are being met, and treating it as such is the same category error as reading a divergence between them as evidence of failure.

**Not measured.** The basis of the "nearly 65 per cent of defence equipment produced domestically" figure — No denominator, population or baseline date is stated for it in anything retrieved here. By value or by count, of annual procurement or of standing inventory, and against what year — each choice gives a different number, and the figure is quoted as though it were one quantity. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: A Ministry of Defence or Department of Defence Production statement of the series definition, or a Parliamentary answer giving the numerator and denominator.

**Not measured.** Realised indigenous content against the DAP category minimums, aggregated across contracts — DAP defines how IC is computed per contract and instituted verification, but no aggregate of verified IC across signed contracts is published. Without it, the minimums are known to be contractual conditions and not known to be outcomes. The stated reason is that the figure exists in some body’s hands but has not been released. What would settle it: An aggregate of verified indigenous content by acquisition category from the Department of Defence Production, or a CAG audit of IC verification.

**Caveat.** "1.78 lakh crore" is stated to three significant figures while ₹46,429 crore is exact, so their ratio is determined only to about 3.82-3.84 and is written here as "roughly 3.8 times" rather than to more places than the source supports.

### 8. Pellet-firing shotguns in Kashmir: the deaths counted, the injuries refused
*Dated 2016-07-08.*

**What this is.** On 7 February 2018 MHA answered a Rajya Sabha question that asked for the number of protesters injured or killed by pellet guns in Jammu and Kashmir. It supplied a month-by-month table of deaths for calendar 2015 to 2017 - seventeen protesters killed, and two security-force personnel killed in law-and-order protests - and refused everything else in one sentence: 'Further details in the matter cannot be disclosed in the interest of the national security.' That single table is the only official Indian document located in this stage of the work that puts harm to civilians and harm to the state's own personnel side by side on one page. It is also the only pellet quantity any government has ever published. The injuries, the munitions expended and the metallurgy of the pellets were all refused under the same exemption on the same afternoon.

**What can be established since.** The word 'pellet' occurs zero times in every MHA Annual Report from 2013-14 to 2024-25. Outside the flagship, figures exist and were produced under compulsion rather than published. The J&K Chief Minister told the Legislative Assembly, in a written reply reported on 12 January 2018 - twenty-six days before MHA's refusal - that between 8 July 2016 and 27 February 2017 in the Kashmir division 51 people were killed, 9,042 injured, 6,221 injured by pellets, 782 with eye injuries and 510 hospitalised. That route closed: the assembly was suspended in June 2018, dissolved in November 2018, and the assembly restored in October 2024 is barred by the Reorganisation Act from legislating on police and public order. In the High Court, on 11 May 2018, the Director of Health Services Kashmir filed a district-wise list of persons who had received pellet injuries, and the Medical Superintendents of SMHS and Bone & Joint Hospitals filed counts, natures of injury and procedures; the judgment of 11 March 2020 reproduces none of the numbers and dismissed the petition. Government Order No.61-Home of 2017 enumerated permanent and partial disablement over a 118-day window ending 2 November 2016 - a boundary the assembly's own figure crosses - and sanctioned Rs 0.75 lakh to 54 persons and Rs 0.50 lakh to 10. The CRPF told the same court it had fired 1.3 million pellets, 8,650 tear-smoke shells and 2,671 plastic pellets in the 32 days from 8 July 2016, so consumption returns demonstrably exist. The weapon was never withdrawn: MHA's own most recent authoritative statement describes an escalation sequence in which other munitions are used 'before the use of Pellet Guns', and on 30 July 2026 the Supreme Court declined a blanket ban on the ground that a Bureau of Police Research and Development advisory permits use in exceptional crowd-control circumstances.

**The case for.** The choice was never between the pellet gun and nothing; it was between the pellet gun and a rifle, and the CRPF said so on affidavit to the High Court: 'In case this (pellet shotgun) is withdrawn, the CRPF would have no recourse in extreme situations but to open fire with rifles, which may cause more fatalities.' Before 2010 that was the recourse. The comparison is the state's own and it is stark: in roughly four months of unrest in 2010, policed largely without this weapon, around 112 civilians died; in the far larger and longer unrest of 2016-17, with 1.3 million pellets fired in the first 32 days alone, the Chief Minister told the Assembly that 51 were killed in the Kashmir division. More unrest, more force applied, fewer deaths - and the High Court found the same on the pleadings before it: 'because of use of Pellet Guns even though more protests become violent the loss of life is less'. The lethality figure is not an estimate but the government's own month-by-month series: seventeen deaths across three calendar years. Nor was the weapon adopted casually. It was ballistically tested at the Terminal Ballistics Research Laboratory on gelatin blocks selected to react as human tissue does; within eighteen days of the unrest beginning the Union government constituted a seven-member Expert Committee on alternatives, which reported within five weeks and whose recommendations MHA told Parliament had been taken into account; the result is a published escalation ladder placing PAVA, STUN-LAC and tear-smoke before the pellet gun. Two judicial forums have examined the demand for a ban on full argument and refused it, and the state paid ex-gratia for disablement without being ordered to.

**The case against.** The weapon cannot be aimed. One cartridge releases between 360 and 600 metal pellets which scatter unpredictably, and the most a senior officer on the government's own Expert Committee would claim is that around 90 per cent strike below the waist - which concedes that roughly one in ten does not, at a rate of 1.3 million pellets in 32 days. The eyes are what is above the waist. The consequence has been measured, by government doctors, in peer-reviewed journals, using stated definitions, which is more than any government count offers: in one Srinagar hospital in four months of 2016, 777 patients were admitted with pellet-related ocular injuries, 76.3 per cent of eyes had open-globe injury, and final best-corrected visual acuity was counting fingers or worse in 82.4 per cent of eyes; in a prospective series at the same hospital 10.8 per cent of eyes had no perception of light at all; mean age in both is about 22. Amnesty International documented 88 people whose eyesight was damaged between 2014 and 2017, of whom 14 were women hit inside their own homes. And 'non-lethal' is the state's own word for a weapon that on the state's own count killed seventeen people. The trade-off argument requires knowing how many deaths were avoided, and nobody has ever measured that. What has been measured is the harm - and the ministry that publishes the deaths refused to publish the harm on national-security grounds twenty-six days after its own state government had published the aggregate in a legislature, and four months before the same state's health department filed a district-wise injury list on affidavit. The two bodies that might have produced an independent count are gone: the State Human Rights Commission, which had produced the only severity taxonomy in existence, was extinguished on 31 October 2019, and the Assembly that extracted the only aggregate has been barred from the subject matter since. The one human-rights body that survives may not investigate the CRPF at all.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Contested on the written definition, and the contest is not the one usually described. It is not mainly a values disagreement about proportionality: the state holds the measurements for both sides of its own trade-off, publishes the term favourable to it and refuses the term unfavourable to it.

**On the competing accounts.** The security case's load-bearing quantity is deaths avoided by substituting the shotgun for the rifle. No instrument of any provenance has ever measured it, and the 2010-versus-2016 comparison that stands in for it compares different windows, different unrest intensities and different deployments, on two figures neither of which has an established basis. The critics' load-bearing quantity is persons injured and blinded, which was specifically requested of MHA and specifically refused, produced once to a court and never reproduced, and never defined by any state body on any threshold. So one side's central number is unmeasured and the other's was withheld. Three further factual disputes sit unresolved beneath that: whether the crowd-control SOP was followed, on which the Director General of Police and the CRPF told the same court incompatible things and no forum decided between them; who was hit, on which the district-wise list that would bear on it remains in a closed case file; and what the operative rule actually is, since the restriction the government relies on lives in an unpublished report. Classing this as different weightings would be wrong, and classing it as a factual dispute between two measured claims would also be wrong.

**Not measured.** Persons injured by pellet-firing shotguns in Jammu and Kashmir, year-wise — MHA was asked for the injured and the killed together by Prof. M. V. Rajeev Gowda in Rajya Sabha Unstarred Question No. 511, answered 7 February 2018, and answered, verbatim, that further details cannot be disclosed in the interest of national security. The stated ground is contradicted by the same government's conduct: the J&K Chief Minister gave the injury aggregate in the Legislative Assembly twenty-six days earlier, and the same government's Director of Health Services filed a district-wise injury list on affidavit four months later. A ministry with a month-by-month pipeline attributing deaths to pellets is working from the same case papers that carry the injuries. The stated reason is that release was specifically requested and refused. What would settle it: An application to the High Court of Jammu and Kashmir at Srinagar for certified copies of the counter-affidavits in WP(C)(PIL) no. 14/2016 - the affidavit of the Director, Health Services, Kashmir filed in answer to the order of 11 May 2018, and the replies of the Medical Superintendents of SMHS Hospital and Bone & Joint Hospital, Barzulla. Secondarily, an RTI to the Director, Health Services, Kashmir for the district-wise number treated for pellet injuries year-wise, stating the definition and the unit of count. The registry addressee could not be verified in this stage of the work because jkhighcourt.nic.in was unreachable.

**Not measured.** Pellet cartridges, pellets and PAVA or tear-smoke munitions expended by force and year — The same sentence in the reply of 7 February 2018 refused the munitions sub-question, part (c) of Prof. M. V. Rajeev Gowda’s Rajya Sabha Unstarred Question No. 511. The holder is established: the CRPF stated a 32-day consumption figure to the High Court in August 2016, and a force that can state a 32-day figure keeps consumption returns. The stated reason is that release was specifically requested and refused. If compelled tomorrow no state holder would have a blinding count to produce. The stated reason is that the figure was never gathered by anyone. ### 9. AFSPA section 7: fifty sanction requests from J&K, none granted, and a decision rule nobody holds
*Dated 2018-01-01.*

**What this is.** Section 7 of the Armed Forces (Jammu and Kashmir) Special Powers Act 1990 requires prior Central Government sanction before a member of the armed forces can be prosecuted for anything done in the exercise of the Act's powers. Since 1990 the J&K government has sought sanction in 50 cases. Sanction was refused in 47 and three remained pending as at April 2018. None was granted. When an applicant asked under the Right to Information Act not for the case files but for the procedure, norms, criteria and standards used to decide, both the Ministry of Defence and Army Headquarters answered that they do not hold the information; the Central Information Commission then sealed the case files under the national-security exemption in June 2020.

**What can be established since.** The figures come from a Ministry of Defence reply to Rajya Sabha Unstarred Question No. 1463 of 1 January 2018, corroborated by OHCHR's June 2018 report and by an RTI application reproduced verbatim in a CIC second appeal; the reply PDF itself could not be retrieved and the annexure listing the 47 individual cases has never been seen. On the decision rule the CPIO of the Ministry of Defence answered on 22 February 2018 that the information is not under his custody and should be treated as nil; the Under Secretary (AG-I) that it is not maintained or available in his section; and the CPIO for the Indian Army on 16 April 2018 that it is not available or held with the concerned agency of Headquarters. Both ministries denied custody of the same records. The CIC's final order of 5 June 2020 permitted access to the refusal orders in the 47 cases but refused the case files under section 8(1)(a), reasoning that the facts required a 'dynamic attribution to the meaning of larger public interest' by associating non-disclosure with it, and that 'indisputably, the larger public interest of the State (vis-a-vis its susceptibility to unrest) prevails over that of the alleged victims'. Separately, MHA told the Lok Sabha on 20 March 2018 that there is no proposal to amend or withdraw the 1990 Act. The Act itself is never named in any MHA Annual Report retrieved, and no disturbed-area statement for J&K appears in any of them, while AFSPA 1958 in the North East is reported every year at district granularity as a measurable achievement.

**The case for.** Prior sanction is an ordinary protection for personnel operating under statutory powers in an armed conflict, and it exists so that criminal process cannot be used to paralyse operations or to litigate battlefield judgement in a magistrate's court. ### 10. Two J&K security quantities lost their only legislative route in 2018, and it did not resume in 2024
*Dated 2018-06-20.*

**What this is.** Two security quantities that MHA does not tabulate at all were on the public record only because a Member of the Legislative Assembly asked and a Chief Minister answered in writing: pellet injuries in the Kashmir division between July 2016 and February 2017, and year-wise local recruitment into militancy for 2015 to 2017. The J&K assembly was kept in suspended animation from 20 June 2018, dissolved on 21 November 2018, and did not sit again until October 2024. The assembly that returned is barred by section 32(1) of the Reorganisation Act from legislating on Police and Public Order, and the Lieutenant Governor may make rules prohibiting questions on matters affecting his own discretionary functions. This is not a gap in a series that resumes; it is the termination of an instrument, and it bit hardest on exactly the quantities the central instrument does not carry.

**What can be established since.** Three refusals in the restored assembly are documented, of unequal weight. In March 2025, at the first budget session in seven years, MLAs were barred from asking questions on terrorism, law and order, All India Services and related matters said to be in the domain of the Central Government, against roughly 1,300 questions submitted - how many were actually rejected, and on what individual grounds, is not established. In March 2025 the Speaker disallowed a discussion on the killing of three civilians at Billawar, Kathua, invoking section 32 and additionally a rule barring debate while a probe is pending. On 28-29 October 2025 the Speaker deleted a question asking the reasons for inordinate delay in ex-gratia and benefits under SRO-43 to the families of victims of violence, on the ground that the Home Department falls under the Lieutenant Governor's jurisdiction, and told legislators generally that they cannot ask questions pertaining to departments under the Lieutenant Governor's control. That third instance is a specifically requested figure, specifically refused, with an identifiable refuser, date, questioner and stated ground, on a question that sits squarely inside the security arc. Whether the Lieutenant Governor has in fact made rules under the proviso to section 46(1), or whether the Speaker's rulings rest on section 32 alone, could not be established, and the two are different things with different routes to challenge. A third quantity belongs with the two above and is better evidenced than either. PSA detention figures were answered on the floor of the same Assembly: in October 2010 the Chief Minister, who also held the Home portfolio, told the House that 724 people had been detained across 2009 and 2010, of which 322 between January and September 2010; and in March 2010 the Home Department, answering an opposition legislator, gave details of 334 persons booked under the PSA between 5 January and 14 February 2010 alone. Carry the contradiction with them - 334 in six weeks against 322 in nine months - because it is the state's own inconsistency showing in its own answers.

**The case for.** The allocation is a straightforward consequence of the constitutional status of a Union Territory with a legislature, and it is the same allocation that applies elsewhere: where police and public order are Union subjects the legislature that does not hold them does not question about them, and the accountability route runs to Parliament, where the Ministry of Home Affairs answers and has in fact produced on request quantities that appear in no annual report - the organised stone-pelting series, the encounter counts, the injured columns. A Speaker's ruling that a department under the Lieutenant Governor is outside the House's questioning competence is an application of the statute, not a suppression of it, and one of the three instances was additionally justified on a pending-probe rule that would apply in any legislature. The Speaker himself has argued publicly for the narrower reading of the rule-making power, that it can only restrict matters requiring the Lieutenant Governor's discretionary action and only after consulting him.

**The case against.** The two quantities that make this concrete are quantities the Union instrument does not carry. Pellet injuries and local recruitment into militancy appear nowhere in seventeen MHA Annual Reports; they entered the public record because an assembly could compel a written answer, and there is now no forum in J&K that can. Parliament is not an equivalent substitute, because MHA is simultaneously the answering ministry and the publisher of the figures - the producer and the consumer of the numbers became the same ministry on 31 October 2019 - and the one time MHA was asked directly for the pellet injury figure it refused on national-security grounds. The pattern of rulings since October 2024 runs toward the broad reading of the bar: three documented refusals in twelve months, one of them on compensation to families of victims of violence. And the underlying question of whether the bar arises from rules actually made or from the Speaker's own construction of competence has never been answered.

The assembly, the elections and the restoration of statehood are outside this record's subject and are not scored here. ### 11. The power to hold a J&K detenu anywhere in India, and the information barrier it creates
*Dated 2018.*

**What this is.** A proviso inserted in 2002 barred the lodging of J&K permanent residents in prisons outside the State. It was omitted by executive order on 31 March 2020, on the official consolidated text of the Act. At least 1,122 PSA detenus were moved out of the territory between 2018 and 2023, 408 were held outside at August 2023, and a prison in Uttar Pradesh has refused under the Right to Information Act to disclose even the number of J&K detenus it holds. A challenge to the power has been pending before the High Court since the Supreme Court transferred it on 16 May 2023.

**What can be established since.** The dating of the removal was in dispute across four accounts and is now settled on the official text, with one question left open. India Code's consolidated Act carries two footnotes to section 10: 'Words "in the State" omitted by Act VIII of 2002, s. 2' and 'Proviso omitted by S.O. 1229(E) dated 31.03.2020'. The Act's complete list of amending instruments contains no 2018 enactment and the string '2018' does not occur anywhere in it. OHCHR's July 2019 update nonetheless names a 'Jammu and Kashmir Public Safety Act (Amendment) Act, 2018, 13 July 2018' and records that at least 40 people, chiefly separatist political leaders, were transferred out following it. What the 2018 Act did, and why it left no trace in the consolidation, is not established. The consequence is that the 295 detenus the J&K Home Department records transferring out during 2019, and the 188 out-of-State detenus NCRB records in Uttar Pradesh at 31 December 2019, were moved while the proviso barring exactly that was, on the face of the official text, still in force. The transfer series itself is the only clean year-wise run in the subject: 44, 295, a stated zero, 146, 585 and 52 to 1 August 2023, totalling 1,122, with 408 held outside at that date. It is corroborated independently from the receiving end by NCRB's own Table 2.12B, which shows Uttar Pradesh holding zero out-of-State detenus at 31 December 2018 and 188 a year later - a fourth instrument, official, that agrees. The information consequence is documented: an RTI application received by Agra Central Prison on 29 August 2019 seeking the list of J&K detainees was refused, and the bare number was refused with it, on a 2008 circular of the Uttar Pradesh Administrative Reforms Department and a characterisation of the information as third-party data. Moving a detenu across the territorial boundary moved the RTI addressee with him, to a body with no relationship to the detenu's family, community, courts or press. One detenu died in Naini jail, Prayagraj, on 23 December 2019 and his family was informed only after his death.

**The case for.** The administration's stated grounds are overcrowding, security within the correctional system, and avoiding the mingling of hardcore insurgents with other prisoners, with the 2018 escape of a militant from a Srinagar hospital cited as precedent - and a prison system in a territory with an active insurgency has a real interest in where it holds people it regards as organisers. Inter-State lodging of prisoners is not peculiar to Jammu and Kashmir: NCRB records 7,076 convicts held outside their own State at the end of 2021, with Haryana holding the largest share. The transfer figures are also not concealed: the J&K Home Department produced a five-year year-wise series of them on a single sheet, and the Ministry of Home Affairs tabled the State-by-State lodging in Parliament in December 2019. And the legality of the power is being tested by the ordinary route - a writ petition by the High Court Bar Association, and a constitutional-validity challenge the Supreme Court transferred to the High Court on 16 May 2023 - which is what a system with a functioning judicial remedy looks like.

**The case against.** Since the removal of the proviso a detenu can be held anywhere in India, and 1,122 were moved between 2018 and 2023, of whom 585 in 2022 alone. That puts them and their case files hundreds of miles from their families and their counsel, in prisons where the RTI Act - which is the citizen's instrument - has already been used to refuse even the aggregate number of J&K detenus held, as third-party information. The transfer also removes the person from the only count that would show him: Prison Statistics India attributes an inmate to the State that holds the body, has no interstate transfer table for detenus at all where it has one for convicts and one for undertrials, and publishes only an aggregate 'belongs to other State' column that never names the origin. So the practice makes the detenu harder to reach, harder to litigate for, and invisible in the J&K row of the only official series. The reasons for the amendment were never given: OHCHR recorded in July 2019 that the State was obliged under the J&K Right to Information Act 2009 to provide 'detailed reasons, facts and materials that form the basis of this amendment' and had provided none - and that Act was itself repealed 115 days later by the same Fifth Schedule. And the transfers at scale were made while the proviso forbidding them was still on the statute book, on the Government's own consolidated text.

### 12. The August 2019 communications blackout: four defensible durations, and orders the state refused to produce
*Dated 2019-08-04.*

**What this is.** Mobile networks, internet and landline connectivity in the Kashmir Valley were all discontinued on 4 August 2019, the day before the constitutional orders. Restoration was staged over eighteen months and the duration therefore has at least four defensible values, from roughly 163 days to roughly 551, depending on which restoration is counted as the end. The orders that imposed the restriction were never published, and when the Supreme Court directed their production on 16 October 2019 the Union claimed privilege and then supplied only samples.

**What can be established since.** Every date below is from a retrieved primary document. 4 August 2019: 'mobile phone networks, internet services, landline connectivity were all discontinued in the valley'. 5 August 2019: District Magistrates imposed section 144 CrPC restrictions on movement and public gatherings; the Kashmir Times Srinagar edition could not be distributed that day and could not be published from 6 August. 16 October 2019: the Court records the Solicitor General's submission that mobile and landline had been partly relaxed, without dates. 10 January 2020: judgment. 14 January 2020: fixed line to institutions managing essential services, 2G postpaid in five Jammu districts. 24 January 2020: 2G restored in all districts, whitelisted sites only. 31 January, 7 February and 24 February 2020: the whitelist runs 329, then 481, then 1,674 sites. 4 March 2020: the whitelist is abolished. 15 April 2020: fixed line without speed restriction. 5 February 2021: the speed restriction revoked, on the advice of a Special Committee then in its sixth meeting. The four durations, each with both endpoints retrieved: total blackout to first fixed-line restoration, about 163 days; to any mobile internet in the Valley, about 173; to an open non-whitelisted internet, about 213; to unrestricted-speed mobile internet, about 551. The 551-day figure is the one in general circulation and requires the most care, because from March 2020 there was an open 2G internet on postpaid and verified prepaid connections and an unthrottled fixed line from April. The two counters that publish the figure differ by a day - 551 against 552 - on whether the start is 4 or 5 August and whether endpoints are inclusive. On the orders themselves: not one document in the J&K Home Department's numbered suspension-order series is dated 2019, the series begins in January 2020 after the judgment, and the Department was publishing other orders on the same website throughout 2019, so the mechanism existed and the communications orders were kept off it.

**The case for.** A shutdown is a preventive instrument against rumour-driven mass mobilisation, and the theatre has a documented history of exactly that in 2008, 2010 and 2016. The Supreme Court, in the judgment most often cited against the state, accepted the premise in terms: modern terrorism relies heavily on the internet, which is used to raise money, recruit and spread propaganda, and which 'provides an easy inroad to young impressionable minds'. The period following August 2019 did not produce the mass-casualty street unrest of those earlier years. Restoration was not indefinite and was not a single switch: it was staged, documented, and each stage was tied to an assessment, and every one of the dates above sits on a published order that was retrieved for this record. Asked in May 2020 to lift the restriction outright, the Court declined and constituted a review body instead - which is not what a court that thought the restriction indefensible would have done. The state is under no obligation to publish operational security orders in real time while an operation is running, and from January 2020 the J&K Home Department has published its numbered suspension orders continuously, with publication written into the order's own distribution list alongside the press and the Gazette.

**The case against.** The longest shutdown recorded anywhere, imposed on a population of more than twelve million, and the orders that imposed it were never published - so proportionality review was impossible by design rather than by oversight. That is not a characterisation but the record: when the Court directed production on 16 October 2019 the Union first claimed privilege, then dropped it and produced samples, and the Court held that difficulty 'is not a valid ground to refuse production of orders'. The published series begins after the judgment, and the Department's website was carrying its other orders throughout 2019, so the absence is not the absence of a mechanism. The deepest point is about knowledge rather than about rights: the blackout disabled the instruments that would have measured its own effects. Every tracker of Indian shutdowns is built, by its own written admission, from newspaper reports, and the newspapers could not publish - so the single most consequential shutdown in Indian history is recorded in the most-cited tracker as one row citing a news website, with no end date and a status of 'Active' seven years later. On the official side, the Ministry told the Lok Sabha in September 2020 that 2G was 'not an impediment' to health, education and justice in Jammu and Kashmir, and fourteen months later told a parliamentary committee that no impact assessment is available with it at all, and that neither it nor the telecommunications department has ever conducted any study linking shutdowns to the disorder they are imposed to prevent.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Scored contested rather than partly or failed because the two sides rest on different facts, neither of which is in doubt, and no measure exists on which the record could choose between them. The scoring is not against a launch claim - no objective was announced for the suspension - but against the Supreme Court's directions of 10 January 2020, which is the same construction another record in this corpus uses and declares.

**On the competing accounts.** The security case rests on incident counts the UT administration produces and the Union relays, on the absence of the mass-casualty unrest of 2008, 2010 and 2016, and on the stagedness of restoration. The rights case rests on a duration, a population, and a stated absence - unpublished orders, and therefore no possible proportionality review. Both are arithmetically correct and neither contradicts the other: grant every incident figure the State placed before the Court and the rights case is untouched, because it is not a claim that violence rose; grant the 551 days and the unpublished orders and the security case is untouched, because it is not a claim that the orders were published. They cannot be netted, and not because the netting is merely unbuilt. It would require two terms that do not exist: the violence that would have occurred without the shutdown, which has no control territory and no counterfactual and which the Government has told a parliamentary committee it has never assessed; and the proportionality record, which is precisely what was not published and was refused when the Supreme Court asked for it. Building the common ledger would require the very thing whose absence is the rights case's central claim.

**Not measured.** The orders that imposed the communications restriction from 4 August 2019 — Specifically requested, and refused, on the record of the Supreme Court. Named requester: the petitioners in Writ Petitions (Civil) 1031 and 1164 of 2019, and the Supreme Court of India itself. Specific request: the prayer for a mandamus directing production of 'all orders by way of which communication has been blocked in State of Jammu and Kashmir', and the Court's own order directing production. Date: 16 October 2019. Refusal: the Solicitor General claimed privilege over the orders at that hearing; the claim was subsequently dropped and only sample orders were produced, on a plea of difficulty, which the Court held 'is not a valid ground to refuse production of orders before the Court'. The stated reason moved from privilege to administrative difficulty, and those are different claims about different facts; what is contested is not whether the orders existed - samples were produced - but whether difficulty justifies non-production, which the Court answered. The stated reason is that release was specifically requested and refused. ### 13. The Review Committee for J&K: findings refused as exempt, ordered released, then said not to exist
*Dated 2023-06-22.*

**What this is.** The Review Committee is the only safeguard the Suspension Rules provide against misuse of the power, and the Rules require it to record findings. The distinction matters and cannot be resolved from the documents retrieved, and this record does not choose between them.

### 17. Loan write-offs and the recovery question
*Dated 2014-04.*

**What this is.** Scheduled commercial banks wrote off ₹16,61,310 crore between April 2014 and September 2024 and recovered ₹2,69,795 crore against those write-offs — approximately 16%. Public sector banks accounted for ₹12,08,621 crore of the write-offs and recovered ₹2,16,547 crore.

**What can be established since.** Write-offs peaked in FY2018-19 at ₹2.36 lakh crore and have since declined to around ₹1.5-1.7 lakh crore annually. A substantial share of the headline decline in the gross NPA ratio from its 2018 peak is attributable to these write-offs and to credit growth expanding the denominator, alongside genuine recoveries which the RBI puts at around 42.8% of the FY2024-25 reduction.

**The case for.** Write-offs are standard prudential practice once provisioning is complete; leaving fully provisioned bad loans on the books misrepresents a bank's position as much as concealing them does. Borrower liability is not extinguished and recovery proceedings continue, so the accounting entry and the legal claim are genuinely different things. Recoveries and upgrades now account for a rising share of NPA reduction, indicating the mechanism is shifting from write-off toward genuine resolution as the cycle matures.

**The case against.** Recovery on written-off accounts runs at roughly 13-18% in practice, so for the overwhelming bulk of the amount the distinction between write-off and waiver is notional. The headline ratio improvement is therefore substantially cosmetic: ₹13.91 lakh crore remains unrecovered, absorbed by depositors and, through recapitalisation, by taxpayers. Presenting the fall from 14.58% to 1.8% as a cleanup without this context materially overstates what was achieved.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other.

### 18. The falling unemployment rate and what produced it
*Dated 2017-07.*

**What this is.** PLFS unemployment fell from 6.0% (2017-18) to 3.2% (2023-24) while participation rose from 49.8% to 60.1%. Decomposed, the improvement is predominantly absorption into self-employment, unpaid family labour and agriculture rather than firm-led hiring.

**What can be established since.** The self-employed share of workers rose to 58.4% and the unpaid-helper share from 13.3% to 17.3%, while regular wage and salaried employment stayed flat at 21-23%. Manufacturing's employment share fell from 12.1% to 11.4%.

**The case for.** Participation rising by ten percentage points while unemployment fell is not nothing — more people were working, and in a labour surplus economy absorbing them at all is an achievement. And agriculture's share rising is a reversal of structural transformation — the process by which countries get richer — so the same data read as a development indicator says the economy moved backwards.

### 19. Indian farmers are net implicitly taxed
*Dated 2014-05.*

**What this is.** The OECD's Producer Support Estimate for India averaged minus 14.5% of gross farm receipts over 2022-24 — the most negative in its 54-country panel — meaning price and trade policy depress farm-gate revenue by more than all budgetary subsidies add.

**What can be established since.** Net producer support has been negative throughout the last two decades, driven by strongly negative market price support: export bans, stock limits and marketing regulations hold domestic prices below international reference levels. The OECD estimated the implicit producer tax at roughly US$169 billion in 2022 and US$120 billion in 2023, against a consumer support estimate of positive 31.8%. India imposed export bans on wheat in May 2022, non-basmati white rice in July 2023, and onion — each stabilising consumer prices while depressing farm-gate prices and damaging India's standing as a reliable supplier.

**The case for.** Food price stability is a legitimate and central objective in a country where a large share of household spending goes on food and where inflation is politically and materially consequential. Export bans during a global commodity shock protected domestic consumers, including the rural poor who buy more food than they sell. The subsidies farmers do receive — fertiliser, credit, PM-KISAN — are real and large in absolute terms.

**The case against.** The policy amounts to a transfer from farmers to consumers of an order of magnitude larger than every farm subsidy combined, and it is invisible because it operates through prices rather than budgets. It is also self-defeating: suppressing farm-gate prices while urging farmers to diversify and invest asks them to respond to signals policy is actively muting. And the episodic bans cost India market share and reliability abroad without addressing the underlying reason food prices are politically explosive.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other.

**Caveat.** This finding disciplines both partisan framings and should be stated before either. Neither 'farmers are pampered by subsidies' nor 'the government abandoned farmers' survives a negative producer support estimate: subsidies are real, and they are more than cancelled by price-depressing intervention.

**What this is.** A growing group of states withdrew general consent under Section 6 of the Delhi Special Police Establishment Act, requiring the CBI to seek case-specific permission. **The case for.** General consent is a statutory choice that states are entitled to make and withdraw, and the CBI retains jurisdiction over central government employees, over cases referred by courts, and over cases where consent is granted individually. The federal design of the DSPE Act contemplates exactly this negotiation. The agency's investigative record in the cases it does take up is not affected by the consent question.

**The case against.** The Supreme Court described 150 pending consent requests as not a desirable position, which is a functional obstruction of investigations irrespective of who is at fault. The states withdrawing consent are overwhelmingly those governed by opposition parties, and they cite a pattern of politically timed federal investigation as their reason - so the federal-comity argument and the agency-independence argument are two halves of the same dispute rather than separate ones. The phrase caged parrot originated with the Court itself in 2013 and was reinvoked by the Court in 2024.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other.

### 21. NEP 2020's three-language formula and Tamil Nadu's refusal
*Dated 2020-07-29.*

**What this is.** NEP 2020 paragraph 4.13 retains the three-language formula while removing every named language from it: 'no language will be imposed on any State', the three being 'the choices of States, regions, and of course the students themselves, so long as at least two of the three languages are native to India'. Tamil Nadu, whose Assembly resolved on 23 January 1968 'that the Three-Language Formula shall be scrapped', has refused to implement it. The Union's own position, stated in Parliament in March 2026, is that 'Tamil Nadu is not implementing it in its entirety'.

**What can be established since.** The word 'Hindi' appears nowhere in NEP's language chapter. Sanskrit is named twice as an option in the three-language formula. The Union restated paragraph 4.13 verbatim in at least five parliamentary answers and added that the modalities are for states to decide, education being in the Concurrent List. Tamil Nadu released its own State Education Policy on 8 August 2025, based on a committee report submitted 1 July 2024, retaining the two-language formula. The single most important empirical datum arrived from elsewhere: Maharashtra, a state that adopted NEP in full, issued Government Resolutions in April and June 2025 introducing Hindi as the third language from Class 1, and withdrew both on 29 June 2025 after sustained opposition. Karnataka - a state with no anti-Hindi tradition comparable to Tamil Nadu's, which implemented the three-language formula for decades - moved to a two-language formula in its own 2025 State Education Policy. Tamil Nadu's resistance to NEP 2020 ran through a second instrument in the same period: twelve bills passed by its Assembly, several of them removing the Governor as Chancellor of state universities, sat with him for between 199 and 1,400 days and were ultimately deemed assented by the Supreme Court and then unsettled by a Constitution Bench (another record in this corpus).

**The case for.** The Union's case is textual and it is accurate. NEP 2020 names no language at all: 'Hindi' does not occur in the language chapter, the choice is expressly assigned to states, regions and students, and the only constraint is that two of the three be native to India. That is a real and substantive change from 1968, which named Hindi and made it compulsory in non-Hindi states, and from the 1992 Programme of Action, which provided 100 per cent central assistance for appointing Hindi teachers in non-Hindi states. The anti-imposition sentence has no antecedent in either earlier policy. NEP is a policy, not a statute; no Act of Parliament enacts it; and the Supreme Court confirmed in May 2025 that it 'cannot directly compel a state to adopt a policy like the National Education Policy, 2020'. Thirty-three of 36 States and UTs have signed the implementation memorandum. And the three-language count itself is not a 2020 innovation - it is the residue of a 1968 national consensus that Tamil Nadu rejected then and rejects now.

**The case against.** Tamil Nadu's objection is to the cardinality, not the content, and on that the Union does not engage. Paragraph 4.13's opening clause is affirmative - the formula 'will continue to be implemented' - and the flexibility clause modifies WHICH languages, not HOW MANY; the curriculum framework operationalises it as three languages with proficiency in the third targeted by age 14. The arithmetic then follows from the Union's own text without any empirical claim: in a Tamil-and-English school the existing two are Tamil, which is native to India, and English, which is not, so the mandatory third cannot be a foreign language and must be an Indian language other than Tamil - and the only language NEP nominates for that slot anywhere in its 66 pages is Sanskrit. 'No language will be imposed' therefore delivers a choice among Indian languages other than Tamil, not a choice that includes no third language. The supply argument is conceded by NEP itself: paragraph 4.12 proposes that states 'enter into bilateral agreements to hire teachers in large numbers from each other, to satisfy the three-language formula', which is a policy admitting that local supply of non-Hindi, non-Sanskrit Indian-language teachers is inadequate. History runs the same way: the 1992 Programme of Action recorded that 'no provision exists for the teaching of South Indian languages in the Hindi speaking States' and that some states substituted a classical language for a modern Indian one. NEP 2020 removed the 1968 corrective - 'preferably one of the southern languages' in Hindi states - along with everything else language-specific, while keeping the obligation, so the burden asymmetry the corrective existed to close is now unaddressed in the text. And Maharashtra settles what the flexible formula converts into in practice: a state that adopted NEP in full, when it operationalised the formula, selected Hindi from Class 1.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Assessed contested because both readings are textually available on the same document and the record does not choose between them: the Union weights the choice clause as dispositive, Tamil Nadu weights the count clause as dispositive, and paragraph 4.13 contains both.

**Not measured.** Whether Tamil Nadu officials agreed to implement NEP in full and then reversed — This is the one genuine factual dispute in the whole exchange and it is narrow: the Union Minister asserted it in the Lok Sabha on 10 March 2025 and a Tamil Nadu member denied it in the same exchange. It is a resolvable question about the content of a meeting and it is unresolved in the public record; the verbatim debate record could not be retrieved. The stated reason is that the figure was never gathered by anyone.

**Not measured.** Any register of which States and UTs have formally adopted NEP 2020 — Ten Ministry parliamentary answers and two press releases were checked and none contains such a list - because there is no formal adoption instrument to enumerate. The nearest official proxy is the count of States and UTs that have signed the PM-SHRI memorandum, 33 of 36. The stated reason is that no agreed definition of the quantity exists, so it could not be collected even in principle.

### 22. Has militancy in Jammu and Kashmir declined since 2019, and on which measure
*Dated 2019-08.*

**What this is.** The government's central claim about Jammu and Kashmir after August 2019 is that militancy has collapsed. MHA's own table shows terrorist-initiated incidents falling from 228 (2018) to 28 (2024), security-force deaths from 91 to 31, militants killed from 257 to 67, and civilians killed from 55 to 14 in 2023 before rising to 26 in 2024. The counting instrument that produces those figures was rewritten twice by the ministry publishing them, in 2022 and 2023, with no methodology note in either report, and the two independent registers that could have produced a competing count - a legislature that could question police and public order, and a functioning local press - were removed over the same period. The record does not choose between the readings.

**What was claimed at the outset.** MHA Annual Report 2024-25 para 15.9, verbatim: 'The security situation of UT of J&K has improved significantly. Organized stone pelting and protests have become things of the past.' The stated approach, from RS Unstarred Q.384 of 6 December 2023: 'The Government has a policy of zero tolerance against terrorism. The approach of the Government is to dismantle the terror eco-system.'

**What can be established since.** Totals computed from the AR 2024-25 rows for 2018-2024: 835 terrorist-initiated incidents, 731 encounters and counter-terror operations, 369 security-force personnel killed, 249 civilians killed, 1,142 militants killed. Infiltration attempts fell from 419 (2017) to 53 (2022), after which MHA stopped publishing the table. Tourist visits are offered as the normalcy proxy at 2.11 crore (2023) and 2.36 crore (2024), and President's Rule was revoked by S.O.4484(E) dated 13 October 2024. Against that: MHA has published 614, 417 and 228 as the number of incidents in calendar 2018, and 594, 255 and 153 for 2019, with no note accompanying either change; the trend is not monotonic on its own series (2021 at 129 above 2020 at 126); and the Pahalgam attack of 22 April 2025 killed 26 civilians in one incident, more than MHA's entire recorded civilian toll for 2023. MHA Annual Report 2025-26 was not located, so the whole of calendar 2025 - including the government's own classification of Pahalgam - sits outside the primary series here.

**The case for.** Every measure the state publishes moves the same way and the largest falls are in the measures hardest to manufacture. Terrorist-initiated incidents fell 87.7 per cent between 2018 and 2024. Militants killed fell from 257 to 67, which is what a shrinking adversary looks like, and you cannot redefine your way to fewer bodies. Infiltration attempts fell from 419 to 53 and net estimated infiltration from 136 to 14. Above all, security-force deaths fell from 91 to 31 - and this is the government's strongest number precisely because it is the series the state has least incentive and least ability to understate: the dead are named, pensioned and publicly honoured, and no critic located in this stage of the work offers a competing count. The independent press-derived register agrees with MHA on this measure year after year, within about ten per cent (91/95 in 2018, 80/78 in 2019, 63/56 in 2020, 42/45 in 2021). The restructuring objection also has a limit that fairness requires stating: the Annual Report table from 2022-23 onward does include civilians killed in counter-terrorism operations, per its own footnote, so the charge that the frame excludes one side's harm holds against the parliamentary series and against most political usage but not against the current table. And the corroboration extends beyond violence data: 2.36 crore tourist visits in 2024, an Amarnath Yatra completed by 5.12 lakh pilgrims, an assembly election held and President's Rule revoked.

**The case against.** The instrument was rewritten mid-series by the party being measured, and rewritten for this theatre alone. MHA restated the incident series for 2017, 2018 and 2019 in the Annual Report 2021-22 with no note, then split it into two columns in the Annual Report 2022-23 with no note, then revised the civilian figures upward for 2018-2020 with no note - while the Left Wing Extremism table, in the same reports, kept an unchanged header throughout. The fall from 228 to 28 is read in public against a series that used to report 614 for the same year. Neither instrument has ever counted civilians killed by security forces outside counter-terrorism operations at all, and neither separates the two causes it now merges. The count itself is generated at the point of the killing by the force that did the killing, and the body is then removed to a distant graveyard: Amshipora put three civilian labourers into the 2020 militants-killed figure for eleven weeks. The verification machinery is inert - 506 magisterial inquiries since 1990, one completed and that report never submitted, no convictions - and both instruments that could have produced an independent count were removed over the same period: the J&K assembly did not sit from 2018 to 2024 and cannot legislate on police and public order now, the Kashmir Press Club closed in January 2022, and at least seven named journalists were held under anti-terror law between 2018 and 2023. And the trend is not the clean line it is quoted as: targeted killings of minorities and migrant workers rose in 2021-22, and one attack at Pahalgam in April 2025 killed more civilians than the government's own count records for the whole of 2023, in the district and the sector used as the normalcy proxy.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Scored contested on the written definition - the evidence supports more than one defensible reading and the record does not choose. **On the competing accounts.** The government's case is a set of quantities. The critics' case is a claim about the instrument that produced them: that it was restated twice without notice by the ministry publishing it, restructured for this theatre alone, and that the two registers capable of contradicting it - a legislature competent over police and public order, and a local press - ceased to operate over the same window. ### 23. Delimitation of Jammu and Kashmir: one Commission table, three denominators, three answers
*Dated 2022-05-20.*

**What this is.** The Delimitation Commission's final order took effect on 20 May 2022, raising the Assembly from 83 fillable seats to 90 and allocating 47 to the Kashmir division and 43 to Jammu. On the 2011 Census population that leaves a Kashmir seat representing 21,481 more people than a Jammu seat, a gap of 17.17 per cent. On area it inverts: a Jammu seat covers 611.5 square kilometres against a Kashmir seat's 339.3. On the 2024 electoral roll the two divisions are within 1.31 per cent of each other. All three are computable from published official data, and the choice between them is the dispute.

**What was claimed at the outset.** The statutory objective is that constituencies shall, so far as practicable, have equal population, having regard to physical features, existing boundaries of administrative units, means of communication and public convenience.

**What can be established since.** The seat chain, every step read from the Gazette: 111 seats in the erstwhile State Assembly including four in Ladakh, of which 24 were reserved for territory under Pakistan's occupation and never filled, leaving 87 that elections were actually held on. Section 14(3) of the Reorganisation Act set the new Assembly at 107 with section 14(4)(a) keeping 24 vacant - the Gazette spells 'twenty four' in words - which is 83; the Third Schedule was parsed entry by entry and contains exactly 83 constituencies numbered 1 to 83 with no gaps or duplicates, 46 in the Kashmir division and 37 in Jammu. Section 60(1) then raised 107 to 114, which is 90 fillable. Both arithmetics give the same seven: 90 minus 83 and 114 minus 107. The Commission sat from 6 March 2020 to 5 May 2022 and its two orders took effect on 20 May 2022 by an order of the Ministry of Law and Justice under section 62(2) and (3), not of the Home Ministry - which is why the Home Ministry's annual reports do not carry the date. The final allocation is 47 Kashmir and 43 Jammu, with 9 seats reserved for Scheduled Tribes and 7 for Scheduled Castes; the proportionality check on the Commission's own population header gives 9.35 and 6.78, so both round to what was allocated. The population arithmetic, with both ratios taken the same way round - Kashmir divided by Jammu - because taking them in opposite directions makes a widening gap read as a reversal: after delimitation, Kashmir's 6,888,475 over 47 seats is 146,563 per seat against Jammu's 5,378,538 over 43 at 125,082, a difference of 21,481 and a ratio of 1.1717. On the pre-delimitation frame of 46 and 37 the same populations give 149,749 and 145,366, a ratio of 1.0302. Both ratios exceed one, so a Kashmir seat represented more people than a Jammu seat before delimitation and represents more after; what changed is the size of the gap, from 3 per cent to 17 per cent. On area, Kashmir's 15,948 square kilometres over 47 seats is 339.3 and Jammu's 26,293 over 43 is 611.5. On the 2024 electoral roll, derived by summing the Commission's own phase press notes, Kashmir has 98,653 electors per seat and Jammu 97,377, a gap of 1.31 per cent - thirteen times smaller than the population gap, and the whole of the difference is that electors are 67.31 per cent of the Kashmir division's 2011 population against 77.85 per cent of Jammu's.

**The case for.** The statute does not say population alone. Section 60 requires regard to physical features, existing boundaries of administrative units, means of communication and public convenience, and the Commission's own methodology says in terms that it weighted terrain, remoteness and border conditions alongside population. On area - which is what those factors are about - the allocation runs the other way, and a Jammu seat covers nearly twice the ground a Kashmir seat does. On the measure that actually determines representation at a poll, registered electors, the two divisions are within 1.31 per cent of each other, which is as close to parity as any delimitation in India achieves. The reservation of 9 seats for Scheduled Tribes was the first ever in the territory and is proportionate on the Commission's own population figures. And the Commission published the underlying table: population, area, density and seats, district by district, in adjacent columns, so both cases can be made from the body's own document.

**The case against.** On the denominator the statute names first, the allocation moved in the wrong direction. Before delimitation a Kashmir seat represented 3 per cent more people than a Jammu seat; after it, 17 per cent more. A strict-proportional allocation on the Commission's own population figures gives the Kashmir division 50.54 seats against the 47 it received, and the Jammu division 39.46 against 43. The countervailing factors are not measured anywhere: the district categorisation that converts population and area into seats is the Commission's own judgement rather than a measurement, the tolerance band is stated as plus or minus 10 per cent in one place in the Commission's volume and plus or minus 20 per cent in another, and the additional constituency allowed for remoteness was granted under no published rule. The order is by section 60(5)(c) not to be called in question in any court. And the body that drew the constituencies contained nobody chosen by the population whose constituencies were drawn: its ex-officio members were the Chief Election Commissioner and a State Election Commissioner appointed by the administration of a Union Territory under President's rule, its five associate members from the Lok Sabha could by statute neither vote nor sign, and the Assembly half of its associate membership could not be constituted because the Assembly had been dissolved.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Filed contested, and the value is carrying a weighting dispute rather than an evidentiary one, which is recorded here rather than resolved. On the written definition the evidence supports more than one defensible reading and this record does not choose between them, which fits - but the more precise description is that it supports one reading of the facts and three of their weight. unscoreable for want of a stated target is wrong because an objective was stated in the statute; failed and partly both presuppose a single denominator the statute does not supply. This is the another record in this corpus shape - a finding about how a figure is presented and what it is presented against - and it should be reviewed with another record in this corpus.

**On the competing accounts.** Flagged false deliberately, because the surface of this dispute - region against region, census against census - makes it look like a different-facts case and it is not. Both cases rest on the same table, Working Paper I of the Delimitation Commission's own compendium, and disagree only about which column of it is the right denominator. On 2011 population the Kashmir division is under-allocated by 3.54 seats and a Jammu seat represents 21,481 fewer people; on area the Jammu division is under-allocated by 13 seats; on the 2024 electoral roll the disparity is 1.31 per cent. All three computations are exact, all three use figures published by official bodies, and the first two use figures the Commission itself printed in adjacent columns of one page. **Not measured.** The dissenting notes given by the Commission's associate members on the draft proposals — The Commission states in its own compendium that dissenting notes were given and were published with the draft order on 14 March 2022, and then does not reproduce them in that compendium. ### 24. The GST compensation guarantee: 14 per cent on a 2015-16 base, 1 July 2017 to 30 June 2022
*Dated 2017-07-01.*

**What this is.** Section 18 of the Constitution (101st Amendment) Act 2016 commanded Parliament to compensate the states for revenue loss arising on account of the implementation of GST "for a period of five years". The GST (Compensation to States) Act 2017 gave every state a protected revenue growing at 14 per cent per annum compounded from a FY2015-16 base, compensation equal to protected revenue minus actual revenue, payable bi-monthly, funded by a compensation cess on demerit and luxury goods paid into a GST Compensation Fund, for a transition period ending 30 June 2022. The guarantee ran its full statutory life and expired by effluxion of time.

**What was claimed at the outset.** To hold states harmless against revenue loss from the change of tax regime for five years. The Council's Chairperson characterised it at the 7th meeting as a "Constitutional commitment for the Central Government to provide hundred per cent compensation"; the member from Jammu and Kashmir as "an insurance at 14 per cent... even if a State suffered from a calamity".

**What can be established since.** The Council's own 54th-meeting ledger records ₹6,64,203 crore of compensation paid against ₹7,74,441 crore of cess collected to August 2024. The 14 per cent rate was deliberately set above any defensible projection of nominal growth and was held uniform: the Council refused Meghalaya's request for 18 per cent for the North-East at its 8th meeting precisely to hold that line. The same 8th meeting decided that any shortfall in the Fund would be met by "borrowing from the market which could be repaid by collection of cess in the sixth year or further subsequent year" — a decision the Council's own Secretary assured members need not be legislated, and which was accordingly omitted from the Act. In 2020 the Union relied on its absence from the Act.

**The case for.** A single national rate, uniform across states, is administrable and resists rent-seeking, and the Council held the line against a regional exception in its eighth meeting. The 14 per cent was set generously on purpose, to make an irreversible constitutional reform politically possible; the Chairperson said openly that it exceeded growth plus inflation. Payment was bi-monthly and formulaic, not discretionary. And the promise was kept in cash: ₹6.64 lakh crore was paid, in a period containing the deepest output contraction in India's recorded history, and the states received it while the Union's own revenue fell further than theirs.

**The case against.** A guaranteed 14 per cent nominal growth locked in the FY2015-16 revenue ranking of states for five years regardless of subsequent performance and, because it was set above any plausible growth path, guaranteed that the cess would be structurally inadequate in any year short of a boom. That inadequacy was foreseen inside the Council and provided for at the 8th meeting by a decision to borrow against future cess; the Council's own officer advised that the decision need not be legislated; and the Union then relied on its absence from the legislation when the shortfall arrived. That is not an inference — it is Punjab's argument, minuted at the 41st meeting with the minute and page cited. The states surrendered the bulk of their independent indirect-tax power permanently in exchange for a five-year insurance whose economic content was left entirely below the constitutional level, with no starting date, no rate and no base year in the Amendment Act, and which expired without any body having to decide that it should.

**Verdict.** The project declines to score this, holding that the two sides argue past each other. Contested because the evidence supports more than one defensible reading of whether the guarantee was honoured, and the record does not choose. The Union's ledger shows the money paid; the states' record shows a substantial part of it arriving as debt raised in their own name after the Union declined to fund the Fund, under an arrangement the Council never recommended. Both accounts rest on documents that say what each side says they say.

**On the competing accounts.** The two sides count different instruments, not one instrument differently. The Union's case rests on the text of the GST (Compensation to States) Act 2017, which contains a five-year transition period and no borrowing obligation. The states' case rests on the minutes of the 8th and 10th Council meetings, which record a decision that shortfall would be met by borrowing repaid from an extended cess, and an assurance that it need not be legislated. Both documents exist, both say what each side says they say, and granting either side's strongest fact in full leaves the other's intact. No common measure could place a statutory text and a minuted decision on one ledger: which instrument governs is a legal question, and it has never been adjudicated because the Article 279A(11) mechanism was never established.

**Caveat.** The guarantee's five-year life is a regime boundary, not a data break. A compensation series ends on 30 June 2022 with the guarantee; the compensation cess is a separate instrument that continues past that date to service the back-to-back loans. Modelling the two as one continuous series asserts a continuity the law does not have.

### 35. Punatsangchhu-II exported power to India for nearly seven months before the tariff protocol was signed, and no tariff figure has been published for either period
*Dated 2026-04-09.*

The Tariff Protocol for the project was signed on 9 April 2026, during the Union Power Minister's visit to Bhutan, alongside a Methodology for Reactive Energy Accounting of reactive power exchange. Indo-Bhutan hydropower cooperation is dated by the Ministry to 1961, with an agreement on hydroelectric power cooperation in 2006.

**What was claimed at the outset.** The Ministry's framing at the signing: another step in mutually beneficial hydropower cooperation, within an enhanced bilateral institutional framework mechanism covering non-hydro energy, cross-border transmission, project financing, capacity building and institutional partnerships. The 9 April 2026 signings are substantive rather than ceremonial: a tariff protocol and a reactive-energy accounting methodology are the two instruments a cross-border interconnection actually needs to settle accounts and hold the grid stable. ### 36. Asset Quality Review
*Dated 2015-07.*

**What this is.** RBI review under Governor Raghuram Rajan forcing uniform recognition of stressed assets across banks, ending the Corporate Debt Restructuring practices that had concealed the twin balance sheet problem inherited from the 2004-2011 lending boom.

**What was claimed at the outset.** Force honest recognition of bad loans so that resolution could begin, on the premise that a concealed problem cannot be fixed.

**What can be established since.** Reported PSB gross NPAs rose from 4.97% (March 2015) to a peak of 14.58% (March 2018) on the global-operations basis — a rise driven by recognition, not deterioration. Special dispensations for restructured loans were withdrawn and expected losses provided for. The combined loan book of banks under Prompt Corrective Action shrank around 11% between FY15 and FY18.

**The case for.** This was the precondition for everything that followed — IBC referrals, recapitalisation, and the eventual cleanup all required knowing the true scale. Every year of delay would have compounded the cost, as the 2004-2011 concealment had already demonstrated. The IMF and most academic assessment regard recognition as correct and overdue, and the subsequent trajectory to a 1.8% ratio by 2026 vindicates the sequencing.

**The case against.** The execution was arguably too abrupt. Forcing recognition across the system simultaneously, while PCA constraints bound the affected banks, precipitated a credit squeeze at a moment when private investment was already weak — a meaningful contributor to the pre-COVID growth deceleration from 8% to 3.9%. A more graduated recognition schedule might have achieved the same honesty while preserving credit flow.

**Verdict.** The project judges that this achieved what it set out to do.

**On the competing accounts.** The government's case rests on recognition being the precondition for every step that followed, and on the compounding cost of continued concealment. The critics' case rests on the credit contraction that followed and its contribution to the pre-COVID deceleration. Netting the two would require the cost of the concealment that did not happen, which is a counterfactual rather than a measure - unobservable in principle, not merely unbuilt.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* PIB, GNPA of PSBs declined from the peak of 14.58%

### 37. Demonetisation
*Dated 2016-11-08.*

**What this is.** Overnight withdrawal of ₹500 and ₹1,000 notes, ~86% of currency in circulation by value, with a limited window to deposit or exchange.

**What was claimed at the outset.** Elimination of black money held as cash, destruction of counterfeit currency, and cutting off terror financing. As returns approached 100%, the official justification shifted toward digitisation and formalisation.

**What can be established since.** The RBI's Annual Report 2017-18 confirmed 99.3% of demonetised notes returned to the banking system — ₹15.31 lakh crore of ₹15.41 lakh crore, with only ~₹10,720 crore unreturned. Counterfeit detection was minuscule. Currency in circulation collapsed, then recovered past pre-demonetisation levels (₹18.03 lakh crore by end-March 2018). The Supreme Court upheld the process 4-1 in January 2023, with a dissent questioning it.

**The case for.** Digital payments adoption accelerated sharply and durably, seeding the UPI infrastructure that is now genuine world-leading public infrastructure. Income-tax filings rose, and deposit data gave authorities analytics leads on suspicious accounts that conventional enforcement would not have produced. A one-time shock was arguably the only way to force behaviour change in a cash-preferring economy.

**The case against.** It failed against every objective stated at announcement. Near-total note return means either little black money was held as cash or it was successfully laundered; either way the premise was wrong. Counterfeit seizures were trivial. The informal economy and small business absorbed severe disruption with no offsetting gain, and cash returned to above prior levels within eighteen months. Digital adoption was already on a rising trend, so the incremental gain is unquantified. Deaths occurred in queues.

**Verdict.** The project judges that this did not achieve what it set out to do.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* RBI Annual Report 2017-18
- *(T4)* RBI note-return confirmation reporting

### 38. The three farm laws: passage, protest and repeal
*Dated 2020-06-05.*

**What this is.** Three agricultural marketing laws promulgated by ordinance during the COVID lockdown, enacted in September 2020, and repealed in November 2021 after roughly fifteen months of protest. The only complete propose-resist-repeal arc in this record.

**What was claimed at the outset.** Free farmers to sell outside APMC mandis without state taxes, provide a national contract-farming framework, and remove stock limits to attract private investment in storage and processing — raising farm-gate prices.

**What can be established since.** The bills passed the Rajya Sabha by a contested voice vote on 20 September 2020, the deputy chairman declining the division the opposition demanded; eight MPs were suspended. The Shiromani Akali Dal left the NDA. Protests beginning in Punjab and Haryana became sustained encampments at the Singhu, Tikri and Ghazipur borders from 26 November 2020. The Supreme Court stayed implementation in January 2021 and appointed a committee that farm unions refused to appear before. Eleven rounds of talks failed. The Lakhimpur Kheri incident of 3 October 2021 killed eight including four farmers. The Prime Minister announced repeal on 19 November 2021 and the Repeal Act passed on 29 November. The MSP committee promised at repeal was constituted in July 2022 under Sanjay Agrawal, who had drafted the laws; farm unions declined to nominate members and no report has been produced four years on.

**The case for.** APMC mandis in several states are dominated by licensed commission agents who extract rents, and only about 6% of farmers sell at MSP, so the system the laws attacked already bypassed the large majority. Restricting legal trade to notified mandis suppresses prices where mandi density is low, and stock limits deterred private investment in cold chains and processing. Reform economists including Ashok Gulati supported the direction. On this reading a genuinely extractive and regionally captured marketing monopoly was challenged and the challenge was defeated by those who benefit from it.

**The case against.** The laws were promulgated by ordinance during a pandemic with no pre-legislative consultation and pushed through the upper house without a recorded vote. Agriculture and agricultural markets are State subjects and the central laws overrode that jurisdiction. Dispute resolution was vested in the bureaucracy with civil courts excluded. Removing the mandi tax base threatened the procurement infrastructure underpinning MSP in the grain belt, and contract farming favours large integrators over unorganised smallholders. Without a statutory price floor, dismantling the mandi system would have exposed farmers to buyer power with no fallback.

**Verdict.** The project judges that this was undone.

**On the competing accounts.** The government's case rests on market structure: commission-agent rents in the mandi system, and only about 6 per cent of farmers selling at MSP. The critics' case rests on how the laws were made and what they displaced - ordinance during a pandemic, no recorded vote in the upper house, a State subject overridden, civil courts excluded. Economic surplus and legislative process have no common unit.

**Not measured.** Deaths during the farm law protest — The Samyukta Kisan Morcha counted roughly 700. The government told Parliament it held no record of farmer deaths during the protest and that compensation therefore did not arise. No official count was attempted. The stated reason is that the figure was never gathered by anyone. What would settle it: A state-level death registry compiled from Punjab and Haryana civil records, or an official inquiry

**Caveat.** This is the project's only REVERSED record and it must not be collapsed into either available reading. Both 'good reform defeated by vested interests' and 'bad law defeated by farmers' have serious proponents and the evidence does not adjudicate between them. What the evidence does support is that the failure was as much of PROCESS and consent — ordinance route, pandemic timing, no consultation, refusal of a division vote — as of substance: a reform that might have survived negotiation was lost to the manner of its imposition.

**Sources.**

- *(T1)* Farm Laws Repeal Act 2021 and parliamentary record
- *(T4)* MSP committee status reporting
