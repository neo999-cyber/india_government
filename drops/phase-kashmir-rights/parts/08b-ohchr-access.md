Working Group appears to have stopped actively pressing it.

### India's response
The reports record **no response**. There is no positive response, and no recorded refusal — the
annex simply shows a request and reminders with no visit. Contrast A/HRC/60/35 para. 27, which
names Bangladesh, Canada and Guatemala as having given positive responses in the reporting period.

On general allegations the record is explicit. A/HRC/60/35, statistical annex, India row, verbatim
column values: General Allegation sent **2009/2011/2013/2019**; Response: **"No (2009, 2011, 2013,
2019)"**. India has never replied to any of the four general allegations WGEID has transmitted to
it on obstacles to implementing the Declaration on the Protection of All Persons from Enforced
Disappearance.

### Outstanding cases transmitted to India
A/HRC/60/35, statistical annex, India row, verbatim:

> India — Outstanding cases: **444** (of which female: 10) — Total cases transmitted to the
> Government: **530** (female: 13) — Cases clarified by Government: **70**; by sources: **16** —
> Status of person at date of clarification: at liberty 51, in detention 9, dead 23.

The separate summary table in the same report shows India entering and leaving the 2024–25
reporting period at **444** outstanding cases: no case was clarified, closed or discontinued in the
year. The trajectory across the reports I retrieved is 414 (2019) → 426 (2020) → 445 (2023) →
444 (2024) → 444 (2025). The number of unresolved cases has risen slightly over six years and has
been frozen for two.

### WGEID's own statement on Jammu and Kashmir — the operative passage
This is the single most useful paragraph for the parent's purpose. **A/HRC/45/13 (2020 annual
report), section on India, paras. 66–67, verbatim:**

> "66. The Working Group is seriously concerned by the complete lack of progress on cases of
> enforced disappearance in Jammu and Kashmir. This has been compounded by the closure of the
> Jammu and Kashmir State Human Rights Commission in 2019, which had been working on several
> hundred cases of disappearance as well as on the matter of unmarked and mass graves. The Working
> Group emphasizes that investigations should continue for as long as the fate of the victim of
> enforced disappearance remains unclarified (art. 13 of the Declaration), and that enforced
> disappearance is a continuing offence (art. 17 of the Declaration).
>
> 67. The Working Group also hopes that the Government, having issued a standing invitation to
> special procedures in 2011, will soon reply positively to the country visit request, transmitted
> on 16 August 2010, and the subsequent reminders that it has sent."

Two things this establishes independently of the contested 2018/2019 OHCHR Kashmir reports. First,
a UN special procedure recorded in a Human Rights Council document that the J&K State Human Rights
Commission — the body whose limited jurisdiction OHCHR flagged at para. 95 of the June 2018 report
(phase 11) — was **closed in 2019**, and that it had been working on several hundred disappearance
cases and on unmarked and mass graves. The domestic remedy OHCHR had already called inadequate was
then abolished. Second, WGEID itself frames the access problem as the gap between India's standing
invitation and its non-answer to a specific visit request.

## 6. Has India ratified the Convention against Enforced Disappearance?

**Source — RETRIEVED.** United Nations Treaty Collection, Chapter IV (Human Rights), item 16,
International Convention for the Protection of All Persons from Enforced Disappearance,
New York, 20 December 2006:
`https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=IV-16&chapter=4&clang=_en`
— HTTP **200**, 325,641 bytes, fetched **2026-08-03**.

Page totals as retrieved: **Signatories: 98. Parties: 78.**

India's row in the participant table, parsed from the page's own HTML cells (columns are
*Participant | Signature | Accession(a), Ratification*):

> `['India', '6 Feb 2007']`

**India appears with a signature date of 6 February 2007 and with the ratification/accession cell
EMPTY. India has signed but NOT ratified the Convention. Verified on the UN Treaty Collection,
retrieval date 2026-08-03.**

Consequence, which matters for the access question: because India is not a party, the Committee on
Enforced Disappearances has no jurisdiction over India at all — no reporting obligation under
art. 29, no urgent-action procedure under art. 30, and no possibility of an art. 33 country visit.
The only UN body that can address disappearances in Jammu and Kashmir is the WGEID, whose mandate
is humanitarian and non-treaty-based, and whose visit request has gone unanswered since 2010. The
treaty channel is not blocked — it was never opened.

(Incidental but relevant to how contested art. 33 visits are: the same treaty page carries
objections from multiple States Parties, including the Netherlands, to Oman's reservation
excluding the Committee's art. 33 visit competence, arguing that a State Party "cannot exclude as
a matter of principle any visit by the Committee under Article 33." India is not part of that
exchange, being a non-party.)

## 5. India's stated position ON ACCESS — from India's own documents and from UN documents carrying India's position

Four documents, all **RETRIEVED**, in ascending order of directness.

### 5.1 — Note verbale of the Permanent Mission of India, 31 July 2020 (NV. GEN/PMI/353/10/2019)
Retrieved 2026-08-03 from
`https://spcommreports.ohchr.org/TMResultsBase/DownLoadFile?gId=35460` — HTTP **200**, 2,330,426
bytes, 4 pp. It is a **scanned image PDF with no text layer**; I rendered it to PNG at 140 dpi and
read the pages visually. Reply to AL IND 6/2020 (4 May 2020) from the SRs on torture,
extrajudicial executions, minority issues and freedom of religion or belief, "regarding human
rights condition in Jammu and Kashmir following 5th August 2019."

The second paragraph is the single most important sentence I found on India's position toward the
OHCHR Kashmir material, verbatim:

> "The Permanent Mission of India would request the Special Procedures Branch of the Human Rights
> Council to bring the following information to the attention of the Special Rapporteurs: **Our
> response is exclusively meant for the above Joint Communication and should not be linked with the
> remote reports on Kashmir published by OHCHR or any matter raised therein.**"

Read that carefully. India engages the *communication* while expressly refusing to let its
engagement be treated as engagement with the **"remote reports."** India's chosen adjective for
the 2018 and 2019 OHCHR reports is "remote" — that is, produced without access. India is
simultaneously (i) using remoteness as the ground of dismissal and (ii) declining the mechanism
that would end the remoteness. This is the circularity the parent asked about, stated in India's
own words in a document India itself drafted.

The same note also gives India's substantive answer on the remedy question that OHCHR raised at
para. 95 of the June 2018 report (phase 11: the J&K SHRC had no jurisdiction over centrally
controlled forces). Verbatim:

> "This is to inform that Jammu and Kashmir has been reorganized into two Union Territories of
> Jammu and Kashmir and Ladakh from October 2019. The jurisdiction of National Human Rights
> Commission (NHRC) has been extended to the Union Territories of Jammu-Kashmir and Ladakh from
> 31 October 2019."

> "It may be noted that the erstwhile State of Jammu and Kashmir was not under statutory obligation
> to follow the mandate of the National Human Rights Commission of India by sending intimations, in
> such cases of deaths in custody or encounter, in view of protection given in respect of subjects
> mentioned in List II of VII Schedule of the Constitution (State subjects) in the Protection of
> Human Rights Act. But after Amendment of the Protection of Human Rights Act in the year 2019,
> there is no prohibition of exercising jurisdiction by the Commission like in all other States and
> Union Territories."

This is India's strongest documented counter on remedy, and it deserves to be stated fairly: on
India's account the 2019 reorganisation did not *remove* a human-rights remedy, it **replaced a
jurisdictionally hobbled state commission with the national commission that had previously been
excluded** — precisely the exclusion OHCHR had criticised. WGEID's para. 66 (section 3) and
OHCHR's 29 October 2019 briefing note (4.2) describe the same event as the loss of a remedy. Both
descriptions are of the same fact; they disagree about its valence. The parent should present both.

The four access-relevant recommendations, verbatim from A/HRC/52/11, paired with India's answer
from Add.1:

| # | Recommendation (verbatim) | India's position |
|---|---|---|
| 151.37 | "Consider responding favourably to requests for visits by special procedure mandate holders in accordance with the standing invitation extended by India (Senegal)" | **Supported** |
| 151.38 | "Extend a standing invitation to all United Nations special procedures (Vanuatu)" | **Supported** |

| 151.40 | "Accept a country visit from the Special Rapporteurs on torture, the right to privacy, and the right to freedom of assembly and association (Estonia)" | **Noted** |

**This table is the answer to the parent's question 5.** India's position on access is not a blanket
no and it is not a yes. It is a three-level position, stated formally to the Human Rights Council
and published by the UN:
1. Access **in principle** — supported, twice, without qualification.
2. Access **to named mandates whose subject matter is torture, privacy and assembly** — "Noted",
   which in UPR practice means *not accepted* (India does not use "Noted" as a synonym for
   "Supported"; the same document shows India using all three labels distinctly).
3. Access **to Kashmir specifically, framed as validating the OHCHR reports** — **Rejected**
   outright, and it is the only one of the four rejected.

One fairness point that must be recorded, because it is the strongest thing in India's favour here:
recommendation 151.39 was made **by Pakistan** and used the phrase **"the occupied territory."** A
state cannot accept a recommendation whose wording concedes its opponent's characterisation of the
sovereignty dispute. The rejection of 151.39 is therefore materially over-determined and cannot be
read as a pure refusal of access. It is equally a refusal of the framing. The Estonian
recommendation 151.40 is the cleaner test — no sovereignty language, three named thematic mandates
— and India's answer there was "Noted", not "Supported" and not "Rejected".

India's own framing of the underlying situation, from the delegation's statement during the same
review, A/HRC/52/11 para. 79, verbatim:
> "It stated that the Union Territories of Jammu and Kashmir and of Ladakh were and would always be
> an integral and inalienable part of India. Despite the continued threat of cross-border
> terrorism, the security situation had improved significantly since August 2019."

And para. 7, verbatim: "India had taken measures to deal with terrorism, balancing the liberty of
citizens and the security of the State, overseen by an independent judiciary."

|---|---|---|

**Conclusion, established on documents: there is no UN treaty body with any legal power to visit
India.** Every treaty-based visit or inquiry mechanism in the human-rights system requires party
status to an instrument India has either signed and not ratified (CAT, ICPPED) or not joined at
all (OPCAT, OP-CEDAW). This is not obstruction of an existing mechanism; it is the absence of one.
It also means the special-procedures channel — the one that runs on a standing invitation rather
than a treaty — is the *only* possible route to territorial access, which is exactly why the
sixteen-year-old WGEID request carries the weight it does.

### 7.3 — Answer to question 1, consolidated
**No UN human-rights mechanism of any kind has been granted access to Jammu and Kashmir since
August 2019.** Established as follows:
- **Special procedures:** OHCHR's own country-visits database shows zero completed visits to India
  since 27 Oct – 10 Nov 2017 (section 1). A visit to India is a necessary condition for a visit to
  J&K; there have been none.
- **OHCHR missions:** OHCHR's own 8 July 2019 update recorded denial of access (phase 11), and I
  found no OHCHR document since asserting or recording any mission. The 29 October 2019 briefing
  note (4.2) speaks entirely in the language of receiving reports, not of observing.
- **Treaty bodies:** no treaty body has jurisdiction to visit at all (7.2); the only treaty-body
  engagement was the Geneva-based Human Rights Committee dialogue of 15–16 July 2024 (7.1).

**Caveat I must state:** I established this from the visits database, the treaty tables and the
special-procedures record. I did **not** find a single document in which OHCHR states in terms
"since 2019 we have not been granted access" — the post-2019 documents simply do not describe any
access. My conclusion is drawn from the absence of any record of a visit in the databases that
would record one, plus the 4 August 2020 experts' statement that visit requests remain pending.
That is strong but it is inferential in form, and the parent should say so.

## 8. THE ANALYTIC POINT: is the exchange still circular in 2026?

### The circle, stated precisely
1. OHCHR reported on Kashmir in June 2018 and July 2019 **by remote monitoring**, and said in both
   that it had been **denied access** (phase 11).
2. India rejected both as **"a selective compilation of largely unverified information"** (phase
   11).
3. The party asserting that the information is unverified is the party that declined the mechanism
   by which it could be verified.

### Is it still the position in 2026? Yes — and I can date it precisely.
- India's word for the OHCHR reports, in its own note verbale of **31 July 2020**, is **"the remote
  reports on Kashmir published by OHCHR"** — remoteness is the ground of the objection (5.1).
- India **Rejected** UPR recommendation 151.39 (access + accept the reports' recommendations) and
  merely **Noted** 151.40 (visits by the torture, privacy and assembly mandates), on
  **27 February 2023** (5.2).
- The WGEID visit request of **16 August 2010** was still listed as outstanding in the annual
  report of **21 July 2025**, with the last reminder in 2020 (section 3).
- Eleven mandate holders issued a joint statement on Kashmir on **24 November 2025**, and every
  factual claim in it is hedged — "reports of", "reportedly", "allegedly", "around 2,800",
  "around 8,000" (4.7). That hedging is what remote monitoring produces.
- The underlying communication, JAL IND 8/2025 of **5 September 2025**, has **no reply** on the
  database as of 2026-08-03 (4.8c).
- India's most recent note verbale, **24 April 2026**, does not engage the substance at all: it
  attacks the procedure (48-hour publication window, wrong communication type) and the motive
  ("pre-conceived hostility"), and redirects to the treaty-body record (5.3).

**So yes. As of August 2026 the structure is unchanged and each side has hardened its move.** The
one thing that has changed is that both sides now argue about *procedure* more than about facts.

### The strongest case for India, in India's own framing
Stated at its best, and not as obstruction:

1. **Sovereignty and bilaterality.** Jammu and Kashmir is, in India's formal position, "an integral
   and inalienable part of India" (A/HRC/52/11 para. 79, retrieved). The dispute with Pakistan is
   bilateral under the Simla framework. Admitting an international human-rights mission to a
   contested territory at another state's urging would internationalise a bilateral dispute and, in
   India's view, reward the state that raises it. That the sole access recommendation India
   *rejected* outright was Pakistan's, phrased as "the occupied territory", is not incidental — it
   is the whole objection in miniature. India could not accept that text without conceding the
   characterisation.
2. **Sourcing.** India's charge is not that violations never occur but that the OHCHR reports were
   "a selective compilation of largely unverified information" (phase 11), and that the special
   procedures operate on material supplied by advocacy organisations under a 48-hour publication
   clock that "seems to present a *fait accompli* to the country concerned" (24 April 2026,
   retrieved). Notably, the 2025 press release does hedge every figure — which is India's point
   about the evidence, made by OHCHR's own drafting.
3. **Terrorism is under-weighted.** India presents itself as "a victim of cross-border terrorism"
   (24 April 2026, retrieved) confronting an active insurgency, most recently the **22 April 2025
   Pahalgam attack that killed 26 people**. Security measures in a live counter-insurgency are, on
   India's account, necessity-driven and periodically reviewed; the 2026 Annexure I cites AFSPA's
   complete withdrawal from Tripura (2015) and Meghalaya (2018) and from districts of Assam,
   Manipur, Nagaland and Arunachal Pradesh as evidence the regime contracts when the ground
   situation permits.
4. **Domestic remedies exist and are real.** India's answer to the "no remedy" charge is
   documented and specific: NHRC jurisdiction was extended to the Union Territories of Jammu and
   Kashmir and Ladakh on **31 October 2019** — reversing an exclusion that had previously kept the
   *national* commission out — with 24-hour reporting of custodial deaths and 48-hour reporting of
   encounter deaths (31 July 2020 note verbale, retrieved). Articles 32 and 226 give direct access
   to the High Courts and Supreme Court including habeas corpus. The Supreme Court upheld AFSPA's
   validity with safeguards in *Naga People's Movement* (1998) and held in *EEVFAM* (2017) that
   there is "no concept of absolute immunity from trial" for Army personnel. India is a functioning
   constitutional democracy with an independent judiciary; the premise of international monitoring
   — that domestic remedies have collapsed — is denied.
5. **India does engage where the mechanism is legitimate.** It appeared before the Human Rights
   Committee on 15–16 July 2024 after 27 years; it participates fully in the UPR; it has held a
   standing invitation since 2011 and has an agreed special-procedures visit on the books for
   16–25 November 2026. It replied to 27 of 114 communications since 2019. This is not the profile
   of a state refusing all scrutiny.

**The weakest link in India's case, stated fairly:** points 2 and 4 are in tension with point 1.
If the material is unverified, and if domestic remedies are robust, then access is the cheapest
possible way to demonstrate both. India's refusal is intelligible only on the sovereignty ground —
and the sovereignty ground would equally have barred a visit even if the reports had been
impeccable. India has not published a reasoned position on why a visit is refused (5.4), which
leaves the sourcing objection doing rhetorical work it cannot logically do.

### The strongest case for OHCHR and the special procedures, in their own framing
1. **They asked, repeatedly, through the channel India itself opened.** India extended a standing
   invitation on 14 September 2011. WGEID's request predates it (16 August 2010) and has been
   reiterated at least three times on the documentary record. The 4 August 2020 joint statement is
   explicit: "In 2011, India extended an open invitation to Special Rapporteurs to visit, but has
   several requests pending. We call on India to schedule pending visits as a matter of urgency,
   particularly of the experts dealing with torture and disappearances." A standing invitation that
   is never honoured for the mandates that matter is a form without a function.
2. **Remote monitoring was a fallback, not a preference, and its limits were disclosed.** OHCHR
   labelled its own 2018 report a remote-monitoring exercise. The hedged language India objects to
   is the honest signature of that constraint. The remedy for unverified information is
   verification, and verification requires the access being withheld.
3. **The domestic remedy was not merely criticised — it was abolished.** OHCHR flagged in June 2018
   that the J&K State Human Rights Commission had no jurisdiction over centrally controlled forces
   (para. 95, phase 11). In 2019 the Commission was **wound up** — recorded by OHCHR's spokesperson
   on 29 October 2019 and by WGEID in A/HRC/45/13 para. 66, both retrieved — while it was working
   on several hundred disappearance cases and on unmarked and mass graves. NHRC's extended
   jurisdiction is a real answer on paper; on the documentary record it has not produced
   clarifications. WGEID's outstanding India caseload moved 414 → 426 → 445 → 444 → 444 across
   2019–2025, and stood **entirely still** through the 2024–25 reporting year.
4. **Non-response is itself the pattern, and it is measured.** India has never replied to any of
   the four WGEID general allegations (2009, 2011, 2013, 2019). Two substantive replies to twenty
   Kashmir communications since July 2019 (4.8). No reply to JAL IND 8/2025 eleven months on. The
   one substantive 2026 reply engaged only procedure and motive, not fact.
5. **The evidence base is being actively narrowed.** The Human Rights Committee recorded on
   2 September 2024 that Khurram Parvez — the Kashmiri documenter of disappearances and mass graves
   — "was prevented from travelling to Geneva to attend a session of the Human Rights Council and
   has been arbitrarily detained since 2021" (CCPR/C/IND/CO/4 para. 51, retrieved). If access is
   denied *and* those who supply information from inside are detained, the "unverified" objection
   becomes self-sealing.
6. **They have absorbed India's objections and it did not change the outcome.** The 24 November
   2025 statement opens by unequivocally condemning the Pahalgam attack, expresses condolences to
   the Government of India, acknowledges "the security and investigative challenges" the Government
   faces (verbatim from JAL IND 8/2025), cites **India's own Supreme Court's 2024 demolitions
   ruling** rather than foreign standards, and urges India *and Pakistan* to resolve the conflict.
   Every one of India's stated objections about framing was met. The reply rate remained zero.

**The weakest link in OHCHR's case, stated fairly:** the Office's own reporting on Kashmir stopped
in July 2019 and has never resumed. It never had a Council mandate for the Kashmir reports, which
is precisely India's objection to them, and Council members never gave it one. Special procedures
have carried the file since, and they are independent experts, not the Office — a distinction India
exploits and which is legally real. The system asserting a right of access has not been able to
sustain even its own reporting line, which weakens the claim that access is being withheld from an
otherwise functioning process.

### The one sentence the parent should carry
India says the material is unverified; the material is unverified because access was denied;
access was denied by India — but India denies access on sovereignty grounds that would apply
whether or not the material were verified, and it is the only state party to the dispute that has

## 9. WHAT I COULD NOT ESTABLISH — exhaustively

I am listing every gap, including ones that weaken the file, because the parent needs to know the
edges of what is proven.

**9.1 — The total number of WGEID reminders to India.** The annex publishes only the *last*
reminder date. Across the five WGEID reports I retrieved I can document three distinct reminder
dates (18 Jan 2019, 26 Feb 2020, 7 Jan 2022) but there were certainly earlier ones between 2010
and 2019 that I did not retrieve. I did not fetch the 2011–2018 annual reports. **No total count
is established.** Furthermore, WGEID's own annex is internally inconsistent: A/HRC/54/22 (2023)
records 7 January 2022 as the last reminder; A/HRC/57/54 (2024) and A/HRC/60/35 (2025) record
26 February 2020. I could not resolve which is correct.

**9.2 — Whether India ever formally *refused* any visit, as opposed to not answering.** OHCHR's
database labels are "Inactive", "Reminder", "Requested", "Proposal of Dates", "Agreed with dates".
None of these means "refused". I found no document recording a refusal of any specific visit
request. The distinction matters: silence and refusal are different acts, and I can only prove
silence.

**9.3 — Any reasoned Indian statement on why access to J&K is withheld.** I probed
`https://www.mea.gov.in/press-releases.htm` (HTTP 200, 82,719 bytes) and
`https://www.pib.gov.in/indexd.aspx` (HTTP 200, 242,851 bytes) — both reachable via curl with a
browser UA and DoH — but neither is a search interface I could query for an access statement, and
I did not locate a specific MEA or PIB document addressing country visits to Jammu and Kashmir.
**India's position on access rests, in this file, on a one-word UPR entry ("Rejected" for 151.39,
"Noted" for 151.40) and on inference from non-response.** There may be a reasoned Indian statement
I did not find. Phase 11 holds the MEA rejections of the reports' *merits*; those are not
statements on access.

**9.4 — Terms of any agreed visit.** The database shows the SR on the rights of persons with
disabilities "Agreed with dates, 16–25 November 2026". I could not establish whether that agreement
carries geographic restrictions, whether Jammu and Kashmir is within or outside its scope, or
whether the visit will actually take place. No terms of reference are published in the database.

**9.5 — Two scanned reply documents I hold but did not transcribe.** The 26 September 2022
acknowledgement to JAL IND 6/2022 (gId=37210, 423,621 bytes) is a scanned PDF with **no text
layer**; no OCR tool (`tesseract`, `ocrmypdf`) is installed in this environment. I rendered it but
did not read it page by page, and I therefore make **no claim about its contents** beyond the
database's own "(Ack.)" label. The 31 July 2020 and 24 April 2026 notes verbales were also scanned
without text layers; those two I *did* render to PNG and read visually, page by page, and I quote
them on that basis — the quotations are transcriptions from images, not from a text layer, so
minor transcription risk exists on punctuation.

**9.6 — 24 of 27 India replies since 2019 unread.** I established the *count* of replies from the
database's result table but downloaded and read only three reply documents (31 Jul 2020,
26 Sep 2022 ack, 24 Apr 2026). The remaining replies — including a 3 January 2025 reply with four
annexes to JAL IND 10/2024, and replies of 20 Dec 2024, 18 Dec 2024, 27 Jun 2025, 3 Mar 2026 —
I did not open. Some of these may bear on Kashmir; my Kashmir-keyword filter says they do not, but
that filter searches the *summary* text, not the reply.

**9.7 — Why OHCHR stopped reporting on Kashmir.** Established that it stopped (8b); **not**
established why. Candidate explanations (no Council mandate; the change of High Commissioner in
2018; the UN liquidity crisis that A/HRC/60/35 para. 26 cites as having cancelled a WGEID visit to
the Republic of Korea; a deliberate shift to the special-procedures channel) are all plausible and
I retrieved evidence for none of them as a cause.

**9.8 — Pakistan-administered Kashmir.** Entirely out of scope of this file. I made no attempt to
establish access, or its refusal, on the other side of the Line of Control, and the 2018/2019
OHCHR reports covered both. Any parent framing that presents access as an India-only question is
incomplete on OHCHR's own terms.

**9.9 — Route failures worth recording for future phases.**
- `WebFetch` on `https://www.ohchr.org/en/documents/thematic-reports/ahrc6035-...` returned

  behaves like mha.gov.in in this respect.
- `https://undocs.org/A/HRC/60/35` and `https://docs.un.org/en/A/HRC/60/35` both return HTTP 200
  but serve a **language-chooser HTML shell**, not the PDF; `pdftotext` on the result fails with

- I initially mis-guessed India's UPR fourth-cycle symbol; **A/HRC/52/9 is Finland and A/HRC/52/12
  is Algeria. India is A/HRC/52/11.** Both wrong documents were retrieved and discarded.
