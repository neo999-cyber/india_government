# Part 03 — Cesses and surcharges: the divisible pool the states do not share

*Stage 2 (research), `/phase federalism`. Model: opus — this transcript records **claude-opus-5**.
Three delegated retrieval agents were used, capped at two concurrent; the two that returned each
reported **claude-opus-5** for their own transcripts. Written incrementally to disk.*

**STATUS: COMPLETE.** All ten mandated sections carry content.

**The three things to take from this part, if only three are taken.** (1) The **divisible pool as a
share of gross tax revenue** — 89.1 per cent in FY2014-15 to 74–80 per cent since FY2020-21 — is
the measure to use, because it is CAG-certified and immune to every definitional fight in §D.
(2) The **GST Compensation Cess accounts for roughly half of the apparent doubling** of the
cess-and-surcharge share, and it was the states' own money — §D1 gives the number both ways.
(3) The **CAG's audit splits in two**: its Article 279 certificate is definitional discipline
published only by the payer, but its cess-to-reserve-fund findings are **genuine evidentiary
independence** — the one instrument in this phase that produces a fact neither party supplied.

## Findings

**F1. The phase's central number is real, but it is roughly half the size it is usually quoted at,
and it must be stated as a divisible-pool ratio rather than a cess ratio.** The most-cited series —
FC-XV's Table 3.8 — shows cesses and surcharges rising from **10.4 per cent of gross tax revenue in
FY2011-12 to 19.9–20.2 per cent by FY2018-19→FY2020-21**, a near-doubling. **Roughly half of that
rise is the GST Compensation Cess, which was a state entitlement.** Netted out, using the
Commission's own broken-out row, the series runs **10.4 → 15.3 per cent — a rise of 4.9 points and
a multiple of 1.47, not 1.91**. In FY2017-18 the two measures move in *opposite directions*. (CS-2,
CS-3, D1.)

**F2. Measured the other way round — as the divisible pool itself — the erosion is unambiguous, and
this measure is immune to every definitional dispute in the part.** The divisible pool fell from
**89.1 per cent of gross tax revenue in FY2014-15 to a 74.0–79.9 per cent band across
FY2020-21→FY2023-24** (FC-16 Figure 7.4, built on CAG certificates). Award-period means: **89.2
(FC-XIII) → 82.16 (FC-XIV) → 78.32 (FC-XV)**. I verified the FY2023-24 point independently: the
CAG's certified net proceeds of **₹27,69,957.40 crore** against gross tax revenue of
**₹34,65,519 crore** is **79.93 per cent**, against FC-16's 79.9. **This is the spine of the part
and stage 3 should lead with it, because it needs no view on whether a given levy is a cess.**
(CS-1, CS-5, D3.)

**F3. The mechanism is provable from the auditor's own certificate, on excise, to within ₹5 crore
in 34 lakh.** For FY2023-24 the CAG certified net proceeds of Union Excise Duties at
**₹33,820.71 crore** against gross Union excise duty of **₹3,05,362.18 crore — 11.1 per cent.**
Gross **basic** excise duty that year was ₹33,786.92 crore. **The shareable part of Union excise
duty is basic excise duty and nothing else**; the Special Additional Excise Duty (₹1,46,619.61 cr),
the Road and Infrastructure Cess, the AIDC on excise, the Cess on Crude Oil and NCCD are all
outside. Neither Union document states the ratio and both contain the components. (LR-3, CS-5,
PR-D.)

**F4. The Union's own budget calls the Special Additional Excise Duty a surcharge, and whether it
is counted moves the answer by 3.8 percentage points — as much as the compensation-cess question.**
Receipt Budget 2026-2027, note to item 6.05: *"This is commonly known as surcharge."* SAED was
**₹1,42,880.01 crore in FY2024-25**. On my own line-by-line derivation the four defensible readings
of "cesses and surcharges as a share of GTR" for FY2024-25 are **9.98 / 13.74 / 13.94 / 17.71 per
cent** — a spread of 7.7 points, **larger than the entire rise the series is used to demonstrate.**
The honest figure for the question this phase asks is **13.74** (excl. compensation cess, incl.
SAED) and **it appears in no published source**, because no source asks the question that way.
(CS-4, D3.)

**F4a. The mechanism is documented by the Union itself, in one paragraph, in the Budget Speech
annexure of 1 February 2021.** On the Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess: *"Consequent
to imposition of Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC) on petrol and diesel, the
**Basic excise duty (BED) and Special Additional Excise Duty (SAED) rates have been reduced on them
so that overall consumer does not bear any additional burden.**"* AIDC was set at **₹2.5 per litre
on petrol and ₹4 on diesel** with effect from **2 February 2021**; the customs limb runs to
**100 per cent on alcoholic beverages, 50 per cent on Bengal gram, 40 per cent on peas**, alongside
a **10 per cent** basic customs duty on the same pulses — with the annexure repeating *"Overall
there would be no additional burden on the consumer."* **The taxpayer's position was, by the
Union's own statement, unchanged; the states' position changed by the whole of it, because basic
excise duty and basic customs duty are shareable and the AIDC is not.** The AIDC ran at
**₹75,455 crore in FY2024-25, 1.99 per cent of gross tax revenue**, on which the states' 41 per
cent would have been of the order of ₹31,000 crore. **And the auditor records the Agriculture
Infrastructure and Development Fund, with ₹1,51,092.71 crore attributed to it, as "Fund not created
as yet."** This is the clearest single instance of the mechanism in the phase and it required no
inference: it is quoted from the party that performed it. *(Narrow caveat at A-7: the pre-change
rates are not in the annexure, so no per-litre cut of a stated size may be asserted.)* (LR-9,
A-7, S38.)

**F5. The CAG's finding that cess money never reached the funds it was levied for is established at
source across ten consecutive reports, and it is the strongest instrument in the phase.** In
FY2018-19, of ₹2,74,592 crore from 35 cesses, **₹1,64,322 crore was transferred and the rest
retained in the Consolidated Fund**, with the CAG adding that *"the revenue/ fiscal deficit
[was] understated due to the non-transfer."* The **Madhyamik and Uchchtar Shiksha Kosh received
nothing for five consecutive years, FY2018-19 through FY2021-22; the health fund PMSSN received
nothing for four** — from a cess named the *Health and Education* Cess. The **Research and
Development Cess** disbursed **7.73 per cent** of what it collected over twenty-one years. The
**cess on crude oil** has ₹2,94,150 crore sitting in the Consolidated Fund and its fund was
operationalised for the first time in FY2024-25, fifty years after the levy began. The **Central
Road Fund** short-transfer was reported in **six consecutive reports** before FY2018-19 without
correction. (LR-4.)

**F6. Part 01's outstanding retrieval is closed: the GST Compensation Cess was short-credited to
its own Fund by ₹47,272 crore, the Union accepted the finding, and the behaviour changed.** CAG
Report No. 4 of 2020, para 2.5.1, Table 2.8: FY2017-18 short ₹6,466 crore, **FY2018-19 short
₹40,806 crore**; *"The short-crediting was a violation of the GST Compensation Cess Act, 2017."*
Ministry accepted February 2020; FY2019-20 saw ₹24,945 crore transferred in excess; no shortfall in
FY2020-21. The CAG separately objected that booking the transfer as *Grants in aid to States* was
wrong because *"GST Compensation Cess is the right of the States and is not a Grant in aid."*
**Note also: the widely-cited "CAG Report No. 20 of 2020" does not exist** — the 2020 sweep, with
positive control, returns no such report. (LR-4(e), A-11.)

**F7. The disciplining-measure question resolves in two directions, and the split is the finding.**
The **CAG's Article 279 certificate** is *definitional* discipline, not evidentiary independence:
it applies the constitutional definition to the Union's own figures, it re-collects nothing, **the
CAG does not publish it** (no document, no regulation, and the CAG's own reports expressly *reserve*
it in a footnote), and its **only published form is Annex 4C of the Union's own Receipt Budget, for
one year, FY2023-24.** Part 01's reduction — a shared ledger of arguments, not of facts — therefore
**applies**, in modified form: the number originates with the auditor, but the only channel to it is
the payer. **The CAG's cess-to-fund audit is different and the reduction does NOT apply**: the CAG
*subtracts two of the Union's own numbers and publishes the difference*, which neither party
publishes, obtained an admission and a correction, and repeated an unwelcome finding six times.
**An auditor who performs a subtraction the payer will not perform has produced a fact neither
party supplied. That is the narrow gate, and it is the only one in this phase.** (§Disciplining
measure.)

**F8. The Union's own umpire has conceded the cess point twice, in opposite directions, nine years
apart.** FC-XIV, 2015, para 8.13 names *"States not being entitled to the growing share of cess and
surcharges"* as the **first** of four considerations behind the move to 42 per cent. FC-XVI, 2025,
para 7.57 states that the FC-XIV increase *"generated stress on the Union's finances, in response
to which … the share of cesses and surcharges increased over time."* **The umpire raised devolution
because of cesses, and then found that cesses rose because devolution had been raised.** FC-16's
adjudication of the strong version of the states' claim (para 7.35) splits it: *"the claim that
increased cesses and surcharges have almost wiped out the increase … is unsupported by data,"* but
*"devolution as a percent of GTR … did not rise as much as it would have had cesses and surcharges
not risen."* **That sentence is the finding stage 3 should carry, in preference to either side's
version.** (LR-5, LR-7.)

**F9. Every remedy the states asked for was refused; the one remedy granted was a disclosure, and
it has been complied with exactly once.** Eighteen of twenty-eight states asked FC-XVI for 50 per
cent. Three structural remedies were put — inclusion of cesses in the pool, a cap on them, or
compensatory enhancement — plus Madhya Pradesh's proposal for a **Constitutional Amendment
requiring ratification by half the states to introduce *or maintain* any cess**. FC-16 refused all
of them (*"Nor does it allow a cap on cesses and surcharges or their inclusion in the divisible
pool"*), retained 41 per cent, conceded the substance (*"reliance on cesses and surcharges … except
for short-term specific needs is undesirable"*), proposed a **"grand bargain"**, and recommended
that the Union *"every year … disclose the data pertaining to net proceeds as certified by CAG."*
**Receipt Budget 2026-2027 Annex 4C is that disclosure, and it is the only one.** Whether the
Receipt Budget 2027-28 carries FY2024-25 is the test that converts A-1 from a historical absence
into a live withholding. (LR-7, A-1.)

**F10. FC-XV commissioned a study on exactly this question, published it, and adopted none of it.**
The Vidhi Centre for Legal Policy study of August 2018 proposed a **benchmarked ceiling at 11.884
per cent of gross tax revenue**, a three-year review cycle, a five-year maximum term, abolition of
any cess yielding under ₹50 crore, and — the sharpest proposal — that *"[w]here proof of
non-utilization / diversion exists, the cess must be treated as a tax simpliciter"* with the states
given a share. **None appears in FC-XV's recommendations.** The Commission instead built its 41 per
cent on an express **assumption of "no further decline in the divisible pool"** (para 6.25). CS-1
shows the decline continued. **And the study's own ceiling is miscalibrated: the 11.884 per cent it
is set at is a comp-cess-inclusive Budget Estimate spliced onto a comp-cess-exclusive CAG-sourced
series.** (LR-6, D1, CS-6.)

**F11. The same money is counted as both a cess and a surcharge, and nobody has been required to
stop.** The Vidhi study's footnote 502 discloses that the education and health cesses on income tax
sit in **both** its cess and its surcharge tables — which is why the published surcharge share
"jumps" from 2.59 to 6.366 per cent of GTR between FY2016-17 and FY2018-19 without any surcharge
being raised. **The two published shares may not be added, and the study nowhere says so outside a
footnote.** The underlying cause is that Article 270(1) excludes both cesses and surcharges, so
nothing turns on which a levy is, and the Union has no incentive to disambiguate. **"Cesses and
surcharges" is a never-defined aggregate.** (D2, A-3.)

**F12. The wedge between gross tax revenue and the divisible pool is 20.07 per cent of GTR in
FY2023-24 — ₹6,95,561.60 crore — and published Union documents account for about 94 per cent of it.**
My line-item decomposition reaches 18.80 per cent; the CAG certifies 20.07. **The 1.27-point
residual, about ₹44,000 crore, is not bridged by any published document**, and the gap sits almost
entirely on the customs head (₹32,136.74 crore unexplained, 13.8 per cent of gross customs).
**Recorded as an unresolved discrepancy between two Union publications, not as an error in either.**
(A-2.)

**F13. The distributional incidence runs the wrong way for the intuitive alignment of the dispute.**
Bihar draws **72.3 per cent** of its revenue receipts from devolution and grants, West Bengal 53.5,
the all-state mean 42.2 (part 07, from FC-16 para 5.46). **A given percentage-point erosion of the
divisible pool therefore costs the most-dependent states the most, in proportion to their own
budgets — the opposite of the incidence of the inter-se formula grievance, which falls hardest on
Tamil Nadu.** The two federal grievances in this phase point at different states and stage 3 must
not merge them. (§FORWARD REFERENCES.)

**F14. The compensation cess is ending and a new non-shareable cess on the same goods has replaced
it.** Receipt Budget 2026-2027 shows GST Compensation Cess at **nil in BE FY2026-27**, and a new
head — the **Health Security se National Security Cess**, levied under an Act of **2025** on pan
masala — at **RE FY2025-26 ₹2,330 crore and BE FY2026-27 ₹14,000 crore.** **The include/exclude
question of D1 dissolves from FY2026-27, but the level does not fall as much as the convergence
suggests: what leaves the cess total was the states' money, and what enters it is not.** (LR-8,
D1, A-6.)

**F15. The trap fires on this part's central quantity and there is no way around it.** There is no
state-side and no third-party measurement of Union cess collections, and there cannot be — the
states do not administer these taxes. FC-XV Table 3.8, the Vidhi tables, the CAG's collection
figures and the Union's own 6.7-per-cent statement to FC-16 are **four presentations of one
Department of Revenue measurement**. Their apparent range, 6.7 to 19.9 per cent, contains **zero**
evidentiary disagreement; it is entirely definitional (D1–D4). **Stage 3 must not present that
spread as uncertainty about the world, and must not present agreement among these sources as
corroboration.** (PR-A, A-5.)

## Candidate series

**Denominator rule for this part.** Every ratio here is against **gross tax revenue of the Centre**,
not GDP. No GDP ratio is offered as a series, so the instrument's three-GDP-base rule does not
engage. Where a source states a GDP ratio it is reported in prose as that source's figure and is
not proposed as a series.

**The two-series rule the phase asked for.** `cess-surcharge-share-gtr` is given **twice** —
once **including** and once **excluding** the **GST Compensation Cess**. They are separate series
with separate ids, not variants of one. See §Definitional disagreements D1 for why the choice
inverts the finding.

### LR-1 — The constitutional mechanism: Articles 270 and 271

**What the text says.** Retrieved verbatim from the Legislative Department's own edition of the
Constitution as on 1 May 2024 (S1). **Article 270(1)**:

> "All taxes and duties referred to in the Union List, **except** the duties and taxes referred to
> in articles 268, 269 and 269A, respectively, **surcharge on taxes and duties referred to in
> article 271 and any cess levied for specific purposes under any law made by Parliament** shall be
> levied and collected by the Government of India and shall be distributed between the Union and
> the States in the manner provided in clause (2)."

**Article 271**:

> "Notwithstanding anything in articles 269 and 270, Parliament may at any time increase any of the
> duties or taxes referred to in those articles **except the goods and services tax under article
> 246A**, by a surcharge for purposes of the Union and **the whole proceeds of any such surcharge
> shall form part of the Consolidated Fund of India**."

**Article 279(1)**, the measurement rule and the auditor's role:

> "'net proceeds' means in relation to any tax or duty the proceeds thereof reduced by the cost of
> collection, and … the net proceeds of any tax or duty, or of any part of any tax or duty, in or
> attributable to any area **shall be ascertained and certified by the Comptroller and
> Auditor-General of India, whose certificate shall be final**."

**Amendment history, from the footnotes on the same pages (S1).** Article 270 was **substituted in
its entirety by the Constitution (Eightieth Amendment) Act 2000, s. 3, w.e.f. 1 April 1996** — the
amendment that created the single divisible pool of all Union taxes in place of the
tax-by-tax sharing of the old Articles 270 and 272 (Article 272 was **omitted** by the same Act,
s. 4, w.e.f. 9 June 2000). The exception clause was further substituted by the **Eighty-eighth
Amendment 2003, s. 3** (*"not enforced"*, per the footnote) and then by the **One Hundred and
First Amendment 2016, s. 10, w.e.f. 16 September 2016**, which replaced "arts. 268, 268A and 269"
with "articles 268, 269 and 269A". Clauses **(1A) and (1B) were inserted by s. 10 of the 101st
Amendment, w.e.f. 16 September 2016**; the GST carve-out in Article 271 was inserted by **s. 11 of
the same Act, w.e.f. 16 September 2016**.

**What this means, precisely, and where the usual account is loose.**

1. The exclusion is **not** a discretion the Union exercises. It is automatic on the face of
   Article 270(1). A levy that is a cess or a surcharge is outside the pool by operation of the
   Constitution; no Finance Commission, no Council and no court order is required.
2. The exclusion is **not symmetric between cesses and surcharges**. Article 271 carries its own
   substantive rule — *"the whole proceeds … shall form part of the Consolidated Fund of India"* —
   and it is confined to surcharges. **Cesses have no Article 271.** Their exclusion rests entirely
   on the words *"any cess levied for specific purposes under any law made by Parliament"* in
   Article 270(1). The words **"for specific purposes"** are the only condition the Constitution
   places on a cess, and they are the hook for the Vidhi study's proposal at p. 30 that
   *"[w]here proof of non-utilization / diversion exists, the cess must be treated as a tax
   simpliciter"* — i.e. that a cess whose proceeds never reach its specific purpose has failed the
   only constitutional condition attached to it. **LR-5 is what makes that proposition non-academic.**
3. **The 101st Amendment closed the surcharge route on GST and left the cess route open.** Article
   271 now expressly excludes *"the goods and services tax under article 246A"* — Parliament may
   **not** surcharge GST. But Article 270(1)'s cess exception was not correspondingly narrowed, and
   the compensation cess was levied under it. **The one levy the Amendment's drafters put beyond
   the Union's reach is the one they did not use; the one they left open is the one they used.**
4. **Article 270(1A) and (1B) put CGST and the Union's share of IGST *into* the pool.** This is the
   opposite direction and it matters for the honest statement of the arc: GST did not only shrink
   the pool. CGST is shared — the CAG certified ₹8,14,746.30 crore of it as net proceeds for
   FY2023-24, 99.3 per cent of gross CGST (CS-5).

**Objective stated at announcement.** None of these provisions was announced with an objective in
the ordinary sense; the 80th Amendment's stated object was the reverse of the present complaint —
to give the states a share of *all* Union taxes rather than only income tax and Union excise, on
the recommendation of the Tenth Finance Commission's "Alternative Scheme of Devolution".

**Strongest case for the Union.** The cess and surcharge exclusions are the **original design**,
not an innovation: they were in the Constitution as adopted, they survived the 80th Amendment
which was itself a pro-state reform, and they survived the 101st Amendment which the states
themselves negotiated with a two-thirds Council weight. A Union with no levy of its own that it
can direct to a national purpose is a Union that cannot act on a national emergency, a national
road network or a national health programme without the unanimous cooperation of thirty
governments. The Sixteenth Finance Commission accepted this in terms at para 7.47:
*"The Constitution does not permit the sharing of non-tax revenues of the Union. Nor does it allow
a cap on cesses and surcharges or their inclusion in the divisible pool."*

**Strongest case for the states.** The exclusion is a rule about *form*, not about *substance*, and
form is entirely within the Union's control. Nothing in Article 270 requires that a cess be small,
temporary, or economically distinguishable from the tax it sits on. The Union may therefore reduce
a shareable rate and raise a non-shareable one by the same amount on the same base, leaving the
taxpayer's liability identical and the states' entitlement reduced — and the Constitution supplies
no test that this transaction fails. The measured consequence is CS-1: the pool fell from 89.1 to
74–80 per cent of gross tax revenue while the states' *nominal* share rose from 32 to 42 to 41 per
cent. **A share of a pool is not a share of the revenue, and only one of those two numbers is the
one that is ever announced.**

**Different facts or different weightings?** **Different weightings of the same facts, with one
genuine factual disagreement underneath.** Both sides accept the text, accept CS-1's direction, and
accept that the Union may lawfully levy cesses. They differ on whether "the Constitution permits
it" settles the question. But there *is* a factual dispute, and FC-16 adjudicates it at para 7.35 —
whether the cess growth cancelled the FC-XIV devolution increase. Its answer splits:
> "while the claim that increased cesses and surcharges have almost wiped out the increase in the
> devolution recommended by the FC-14 is unsupported by data, it is true that the devolution as a
> percent of GTR during the award period of even the FC-14 did not rise as much as it would have
> had cesses and surcharges not risen as a proportion of GTR."

That is the most careful sentence written on this subject by any body with standing to write it,
and it should be carried into stage 3 as the finding rather than either side's version.

### LR-2 — FY2018-19: the Health and Education Cess, the Road and Infrastructure Cess and the Social Welfare Surcharge (Finance Act 2018)

**What happened.** With effect from FY2018-19 the Union (i) replaced the 3 per cent Education Cess
on income and corporation tax (2 per cent Primary + 1 per cent Secondary and Higher) with a
**4 per cent Health and Education Cess**; (ii) replaced the Additional Duty of Excise (Road Cess)
with the **Road and Infrastructure Cess**; and (iii) replaced the Education Cess and Secondary and
Higher Education Cess on customs with a **Social Welfare Surcharge**. The Receipt Budget 2026-2027
carries the first of these in its own note (S2): *"'Health and Education Cess' @4% of income-tax
(including surcharge) is levied w.e.f. F. Y. 2018-19 onwards."* — and note the base: **the cess is
charged on income tax *inclusive of surcharge*, so the two non-shareable instruments compound.**

**The quantity.** This is the largest single-year step in the whole series. FC-XV Table 3.8 (S18):
the **direct-tax** cess-and-surcharge component goes from **₹53,433 crore in FY2017-18 to
₹1,45,802 crore in FY2018-19 — a 2.73× jump in one year**, and total cesses and surcharges from
13.9 to 19.9 per cent of GTR. The CAG's own reading (Report No. 4 of 2020, para 2.5.3, retrieved)
puts FY2018-19 Health and Education Cess collection at **₹41,309.07 crore**.

**Strongest case for.** Rate simplification: three cesses became one, at a rate 1 point higher, on
a base the Union was entitled to tax anyway; the Social Welfare Surcharge likewise consolidated two
customs cesses. The Union's revenue need in FY2018-19 was real and the FC-XIV award had just raised
devolution by ten percentage points.

**Strongest case against.** **All three of these are replacements, not new levies — and every one
of them replaced a non-shareable instrument with a *larger* non-shareable instrument.** The
one-point increase in the education cess was not accompanied by any reduction in the basic rate.
And the CAG establishes (LR-5) that in the same year *"[n]o expenditure was envisaged for the
health sector out of the cess, nor was any dedicated fund created for the purpose"*, and that for
the Social Welfare Surcharge *"no dedicated fund was created"* either. **A cess is constitutionally
a levy "for specific purposes"; the auditor found, in the year of its introduction, that one of
its two named purposes had no fund and no envisaged expenditure.**

**Different facts or different weightings?** **Different facts.** The Union's account of FY2018-19
is a consolidation; the CAG's audited account is that the health limb had no fund. Those are not
two views of one fact.

### LR-3 — The Union's own accounts show 88.9 per cent of Union excise duty outside the divisible pool

**What happened.** By FY2023-24 the composition of Union excise duty had been reorganised so
completely that basic excise duty — the only shareable component — was **₹33,786.92 crore out of
₹3,05,362.18 crore of gross Union excise duty, 11.1 per cent.** The rest was Special Additional
Excise Duty (₹1,46,619.61 cr), the Road and Infrastructure Cess, the AIDC on excise, the Cess on
Crude Oil and NCCD.

**The instrument that proves it is the auditor's.** The CAG's Article 279 certificate for FY2023-24
(Receipt Budget 2026-2027, Annex 4C, S2) puts certified **net proceeds of Union Excise Duties at
₹33,820.71 crore** — within ₹34 crore of gross basic excise duty. Working the subtraction the
other way: 3,05,362.18 − 146,619.61 (SAED) − 117,131.11 (DoR cesses) − (−16.01) − 7,812.25 (NCCD) =
**33,815.22**, against certified 33,820.71. **The excise column closes to about ₹5 crore in
34 lakh.** This is the single cleanest arithmetic demonstration of the mechanism in the part, and
it comes from adding two Union documents to one auditor's certificate, none of which was produced
to make this point.

**Strongest case for the Union.** Petroleum taxation is the Union's principal remaining
non-GST lever and it is used counter-cyclically — SAED on petrol and diesel is the windfall levy
introduced in July 2022 on producers' and refiners' excess margins, an instrument states have not
asked to share the *downside* of. The states levy their own VAT on the same litre of fuel, without
sharing it with the Union or with each other.

**Strongest case for the states.** Union excise duty is a **Union List entry 84** tax and
Article 270(1) begins *"All taxes and duties referred to in the Union List"*. The presumption is
that it is shared. Reducing that presumption from a whole to a ninth is not a use of the
exception — it is a reversal of the rule and the exception. And the specific composition is not
economically motivated: SAED, RIC and AIDC on excise are all levied per litre on the same petrol
and the same diesel as basic excise duty, at the same point, on the same assessee.

**Different facts or different weightings?** **Different weightings.** No one disputes the
composition; the Union has published it, line by line, in every Receipt Budget.

₹95,081 cr against ₹54,275 cr (**short ₹40,806 cr**). Verbatim: *"The short-crediting was a
violation of the GST Compensation Cess Act, 2017. The amount by which the cess was short credited
was also retained in the CFI."* The Ministry **accepted** the finding in February 2020. A second
finding in the same paragraph: the Ministry booked the transfer under major head 3601 *Transfer of
Grants in aid to States* rather than 2047 — the CAG's objection being that *"GST Compensation Cess
is the right of the States and is not a Grant in aid."* Follow-through in Report No. 7 of 2021
para 2.3.4: FY2019-20 saw ₹24,945 cr transferred **in excess** of collections, leaving
**₹22,327 crore** of past-year cess still untransferred as at 31 March 2020; Report No. 31 of 2022
records no shortfall in FY2020-21. **The correction is as important as the finding: the auditor's
objection was acted on.**

**(f) Cess on crude oil.** Report No. 6 of 2026 para 3.3.1: since FY1974-75, ₹3,12,782 crore
collected and **₹2,94,150 crore retained in the CFI as at 31 March 2025**; the Oil Industry
Development Fund was operationalised **for the first time in FY2024-25** — fifty years after the
cess began. Report No. 7 of 2021 para 2.6.3 records that nothing had been transferred to OIDB
since FY1991-92.

**(g) Clean Energy / Clean Environment Cess.** Report No. 44 of 2017 para 2.3.4:
FY2010-11→FY2016-17, ₹53,967.23 cr collected, ₹15,483.21 cr transferred to the National Clean
Energy Fund, **short ₹38,484.02 cr (71.31 per cent)**.

**The Union's defence, in its own words, and the auditor's answer.** Report No. 21 of 2023
para 3.6.1 A records the Ministry of Finance's position: the purpose of such funds is to ensure use
*"over a period of years, but not necessarily, within each FY."* The CAG's rejoinder in the same
paragraph: *"Non / short transfer of the collected levy to the dedicated Reserve Fund defeats the
very purpose of imposing such additional tax."* The Public Accounts Committee (17th Lok Sabha),
**69th Report, August 2023**, recommended that proceeds *"should be credited to the reserve funds
regularly"* — quoted in CAG Reports No. 16 of 2025 and No. 6 of 2026. *(The PAC report itself is
known here only through the CAG's quotation of it — **T4 for that reason**, see §Sources NOT
retrieved.)*

**Strongest case for the Union.** A reserve fund in the Public Account is a **cash-management
device, not a trust**. Transferring cash into it before the implementing ministry can spend it
achieves nothing except a larger idle balance, and the expenditure the cess was raised for was in
fact incurred — the CAG itself records for FY2018-19 that ₹11,631.87 crore was *"spent directly on
MUSK-intended heads"* from gross budgetary support even though nothing went through MUSK. On this
account the finding is an **accounting-route objection**, not a diversion of money. And the delays
have a stated administrative cause the CAG records without contradicting: *"non-finalisation of its
accounting procedure."*

**Strongest case for the states.** Two answers. First, the Union's defence proves too much: if the
fund is only a routing device and the spending happens anyway from general revenues, then the cess
is **indistinguishable from a general tax**, and the "specific purposes" that Article 270(1)
requires as the condition of its non-shareability have no operative content. The levy is
non-shareable because it is earmarked and unearmarked in practice. Second, the CAG's own finding is
not merely about routing: *"the revenue/ fiscal deficit understated due to the non-transfer of
these amounts."* Cash retained in the Consolidated Fund is cash available to finance the general
deficit — which is precisely the use a cess is constitutionally forbidden to be levied for.

**Different facts or different weightings?** **Different weightings, on facts the Union does not
contest and in several instances has formally accepted.** The Ministry accepted the ₹47,272 crore
GST finding. The Ministry's own MUSK figure and the Union Government Finance Accounts figure differ
by ₹1.02 lakh crore and the CAG has asked it to reconcile them — that one *is* a factual dispute,
and it is a dispute **between two Union sources**, not between the Union and the states.

### LR-5 — The Fourteenth Finance Commission concedes the cess point, 2015

**What happened.** FC-XIV recommended raising the states' share from 32 to 42 per cent. Para 8.13,
retrieved verbatim (S20), lists the four considerations it factored in, and the **first** is:

> "(i) States not being entitled to the growing share of cess and surcharges in the revenues of the
> Union Government;"

**Why this is a first-class ledger record.** The Finance Commission is the Union's own
constitutional umpire, appointed by the President under Article 280. **In 2015 it named the cess
mechanism, in its own report, as the leading reason for a ten-point increase in devolution.** That
is not a state's complaint relayed; it is the arbiter's finding. Part 07 flagged this forward and
it is confirmed here at source, together with its surroundings: para 8.12 records that FY2012-13
aggregate resource flow was 50.4 per cent of the Union's gross revenues, 57.1 per cent of GTR and
63.9 per cent of the divisible pool, and that *"there is little scope to increase the share of
aggregate transfers"*; para 8.14 **rejects** the states' request for a minimum guaranteed
devolution.

**The measured sequel, and it cuts the other way.** FC-16 para 7.57 states that the FC-XIV increase
*"generated stress on the Union's finances, in response to which it's share in majority of CSS to
non-NEH States was brought down to 60 per cent immediately and **the share of cesses and surcharges
increased over time**."* **The Union's own umpire, nine years later, describes the cess growth as a
response to the devolution increase that the cess growth had been cited to justify.** Non-FC
transfers fell from 22.4 to 16.4 per cent between FY2014-15 and FY2015-16. Whether that is a
vindication of the states (the Union clawed back what it was ordered to give) or of the Union (a
ten-point award was not affordable and the adjustment had to fall somewhere) is a judgement, and
both readings are supported by the same sentence. → part 04 owns the CSS limb.

### LR-6 — The Fifteenth Finance Commission was asked to act on cesses and did not

**What happened.** FC-XV recorded the problem repeatedly and **made no recommendation about it.**
The retrieving agent read all 100 occurrences of "cess"/"surcharge" in Volume I and the entire
Chapter 5 recommendation list (pp. 147–148). What exists:

- para 3.63: *"FC-XIV had highlighted the somewhat problematic growing share of cesses and
  surcharges in Union revenues."*
- para 2.12: cesses and surcharges *"amounting to about 15 per cent of its gross revenues, reducing
  the proportion of Union revenues eligible for transfers to States from the divisible pool."*
- para 6.15, the states' memoranda: *"State Governments also represented that the Union has
  significantly shrunk the divisible pool over time through excessive and rising cesses and
  surcharges"*, and asked the Commission to *"work out a mechanism so that the 50:50 division
  should objectively account for the cess and surcharges currently retained by the Union."*
- para 6.25, the answer: share gross revenue receipts *"in about equal ratio between the Union and
  States, **while assuming no further decline in the divisible pool as a proportion of gross revenue
  receipts**"*, delivered as 41 per cent.
- para 11.46: on defence, *"we have also desisted from recommending any new or additional cess or
  surcharge for this purpose."*

**The finding.** FC-XV **commissioned a dedicated study** on cesses and surcharges (S17, Vidhi,
August 2018), which recommended a benchmarked ceiling at **11.884 per cent of GTR** — the FY2018-19
BE level, described in the study as *"the highest recorded percentage of cess collected as a
percentage of gross tax revenue from 2002"* — plus a three-year review cycle, a five-year maximum
term, abolition of any cess yielding under ₹50 crore, and the proposition that a diverted cess
*"must be treated as a tax simpliciter"* with the states given a share. **None of it appears in
FC-XV's recommendations.** The Commission instead built its 41 per cent on an **assumption** that
the erosion would not continue. CS-1 shows it continued: 81.2 (FY2018-19) → 74.0 (FY2020-21) →
79.9 (FY2023-24).

**Not an absence in the "not published" sense** — the study is published, on the Commission's own
site, and its non-adoption is visible precisely because the Commission published it. That is a
point in the Commission's favour, not against it.

### LR-7 — The Sixteenth Finance Commission: 41 per cent retained, cap and inclusion refused, disclosure ordered

**What happened.** FC-16, reporting for FY2026-27→FY2030-31, retained the states' share at
**41 per cent** and refused both structural remedies the states asked for, while ordering a
transparency remedy the states had not asked for.

**The states' asks, as FC-16 records them** (paras 7.36–7.39, S21). **Eighteen of twenty-eight
states asked for 50 per cent.** Exceptions: **Sikkim** (40 per cent if the pool becomes Gross
Revenue Receipts, *or* 50 per cent if the pool includes *"cesses, surcharges and cost of collection
beyond 20 per cent of GTR"*); **Madhya Pradesh** (48 if the pool is the entire GTR, 40.7 if entire
GRR); **Nagaland** 48; **Assam** 45; **Himachal Pradesh** 41. Para 7.37: *"Many submissions —
including those from Gujarat, Karnataka, Kerala, Madhya Pradesh, Tamil Nadu and Uttar Pradesh —
highlighted the declining share of the divisible pool in the gross tax revenues of the Union."*
Three remedies proposed: **(i)** inclusion of cesses and surcharges in the divisible pool;
**(ii)** a cap as a percentage of GTR with any excess transferred into the pool; **(iii)**
compensatory enhancement of the states' share if they stay excluded. Para 7.38: **Telangana** —
transfer them to a non-lapsable Infrastructure Development Fund for Backward States; **Madhya
Pradesh** — a **Constitutional Amendment requiring ratification by at least 50 per cent of states
to introduce *or maintain* any cess or surcharge**; **Uttar Pradesh** — review once the objective
is served.

**The Union's counter, para 7.45:** *"Excluding GST compensation cess, cesses comprised **6.7 per
cent of gross revenue** during the FC-15 period — less than the FC-13's 7.0 per cent."* Para 7.43
records that the Union *"views this as a settled issue and seeks an end to the debate on it."*
**Note the construction: a *gross revenue* denominator (tax plus non-tax), compensation cess
excluded, cesses only and surcharges omitted. On that construction the share is falling.** See D4.

**The Commission's answers.**
- para 7.47: *"The Constitution does not permit the sharing of non-tax revenues of the Union. Nor
  does it allow a cap on cesses and surcharges or their inclusion in the divisible pool."*
- para 7.58, conceding the substance: reliance on cesses and surcharges *"except for short-term
  specific needs is undesirable"* — efficiency requires broad-based taxation while these apply to
  *"a specific commodity or a specific group of taxpayers"*, and long-run reliance *"impedes tax
  reform."*
- para 7.67, the proposed settlement: *"a **grand bargain** would have to be struck between the
  Union and States in which the Union would agree to fold a large part of the revenue from cesses
  and surcharges into regular taxes and States would agree to a smaller share in the resulting
  larger divisible pool, with no loss of revenues to either side."*
- **Chapter 16, Summary of Recommendations, item 2 — the operative one for this part:** *"In order
  to bring in more transparency about the divisible pool and the actual devolution, we recommend
  that, every year, the Union Government disclose the data pertaining to net proceeds as certified
  by CAG under Article 279. (paragraph 7.30)"* Para 7.30 adds: *"In the spirit of transparency and
  to put the concerns of States to rest…"*
- item 3: *"We recommend that the States' share in the divisible pool is retained at 41 per cent."*

**This recommendation has been complied with, once, and I retrieved the compliance.** Annex 4C of
the Receipt Budget 2026-2027 (S2, CS-5) is the first such disclosure, for FY2023-24. **It is the
only year that exists.** See LR-8 and §Disciplining measure.

**Different facts or different weightings?** Here, uniquely in this part, **the two sides genuinely
differ on facts, because they use different denominators and different inclusion sets — and FC-16
adjudicates by picking a third.** The states measure cesses and surcharges against GTR
(rising, CS-2/CS-3); the Union measures cesses only against gross revenue excluding the
compensation cess (falling, 7.0 → 6.7); FC-16 measures the divisible pool against GTR (falling,
89.2 → 78.3) and finds for the states on direction while finding against them on remedy.

### LR-8 — The successor instrument: the Health Security se National Security Cess Act, 2025

**What happened.** The GST Compensation Cess ended and a **new cess on the same category of
goods appeared in its place.** Receipt Budget 2026-2027 (S2) carries, at a new head 9.03 under
*Other Taxes and Duties on Commodities and Services* (major head 0045):

> "Health Security se National Security Cess is leviable vide the **Health Security se National
> Security Cess Act, 2025** on pan masala and any other goods which may be notified by the Central
> Government for the purpose of National Security & Public Health. The cess will be levied on the
> machinery installed or other processes undertaken by which specified goods are manufactured or
> produced…"

**The quantities, from the same statement:** RE FY2025-26 **₹2,330 crore**; **BE FY2026-27
₹14,000 crore**. In the same table the **GST Compensation Cess** shows RE FY2025-26 ₹88,000 crore
against BE ₹1,67,110 crore, and **BE FY2026-27 of "…" — nil.**

**Why this belongs in the ledger.** Part 01 established the compensation cess as the compensation
instrument and its expiry. **What replaces it, on the same goods, is another cess — and this one is
not earmarked to the states.** The compensation cess was legally a cess but economically a state
entitlement; its successor is a cess in both senses. **The FY2026-27 Budget is the first year in
which the phase's arithmetic changes character: the compensation cess leaves the "cess" total and
the states lose nothing by its departure, while a new non-shareable cess enters and they lose all
of it.** The FY2026-27 BE is small (0.32 per cent of GTR) but it is the beginning of a series and
stage 3 should mark it as one.

*Retrieved status: the Receipt Budget's description and both figures are T1 and retrieved. **The
Act itself is not retrieved here** — see §Sources NOT retrieved and A-6.*

### LR-9 — The Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess, FY2021-22: the mechanism in its clearest single form

**What is established here, at T1, without any further retrieval.**

The AIDC exists in **two limbs** and the Receipt Budget 2026-2027 (S2) states the statutory basis of
each in its own notes:

> **5.01.08** — *"Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess is levied as **a duty of customs**
> vide the **Finance Act, 2021** for the purposes of financing the agriculture infrastructure and
> other development expenditure."*
>
> **6.07.11** — *"Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess is levied as **an additional duty
> of excise** vide the **Finance Act, 2021** on the goods specified in the Seventh Schedule…"*

**The collections, from the Receipt Budget tax-revenue statements (S2–S6), ₹ crore, Actuals:**

| FY | AIDC (customs) | AIDC (excise) | **total** |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2020-21 | – | – | – (levy begins FY2021-22) |
| 2021-22 (RE) | 24,450.00 | 48,920.00 | **73,370.00** |
| 2022-23 | 23,132.87 | 51,009.16 | **74,142.03** |
| 2023-24 | 27,145.54 | 53,778.06 | **80,923.60** |
| 2024-25 | 21,090.81 | 54,364.26 | **75,455.07** |
| 2025-26 (RE) | 27,330.00 | 54,250.00 | **81,580.00** |
| 2026-27 (BE) | 29,950.00 | 55,490.00 | **85,440.00** |

**₹75,455 crore in FY2024-25 — 1.99 per cent of gross tax revenue, none of it shareable, from a
levy that did not exist five years earlier.** *(FY2021-22 is a Revised Estimate: the AIDC carries
no Actual in any Receipt Budget I could retrieve, because Receipt Budget 2023-24 — the volume
carrying FY2021-22 Actuals — would not download intact; see §Sources NOT retrieved. The
FY2021-22 RE is taken from Receipt Budget 2022-23, items 5.01.11 and 6.07.11.)*

**A retrieval observation worth recording.** The **Receipt Budget 2021-2022 itself — presented
1 February 2021, the day the AIDC was announced — carries no AIDC line at all** (checked line by
line, S7). Its BE for **Basic Excise Duties** is **₹47,500 crore**, against a RE FY2020-21 of
₹47,750 crore and an Actual FY2019-20 of ₹61,779.31 crore; its BE for the **Road and Infrastructure
Cess** on motor spirit and HSD is **₹1,98,000 crore** against RE FY2020-21 of ₹2,24,000 crore.
**The budget document that introduced the AIDC does not show it, and the first Receipt Budget in
which it appears is the following year's.** That is a reporting-base fact about when the instrument
becomes visible in the series, not evidence of concealment — the levy was in the Finance Bill, not
the Receipt Budget — but it means any series built from Receipt Budgets alone begins the AIDC a
year late.

**And the auditor's finding on where it went.** CAG Report No. 4 of 2025, **para 3.3.1,
Figure 3.3** (S31) records, among four reserve funds examined for FY2018-19→FY2022-23, the
**Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Fund** with **₹1,51,092.71 crore** attributed to it
and the status **"Fund not created as yet."** **A cess levied for a specific purpose, running at
₹75,000+ crore a year, whose fund the auditor records as not having been created.** That is the
Vidhi study's *tax simpliciter* condition (LR-6) met on its face — a cess whose proceeds cannot
have reached the "specific purposes" that Article 270(1) makes the sole condition of its
non-shareability, because the fund for those purposes did not exist. It is established at T1 from
the auditor, against the Union, on the Union's accounts.

**THE OFFSET, IN THE UNION'S OWN WORDS — retrieved and established at T1.**

The **Budget 2021-2022 Budget Speech, Annexure to Part B** (S38, retrieved at
`https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/budget2021-22/doc/budget_speech.pdf`), item 5 of the excise
annexure, verbatim:

> "Consequent to imposition of Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC) on petrol
> and diesel, the **Basic excise duty (BED) and Special Additional Excise Duty (SAED) rates have
> been reduced on them so that overall consumer does not bear any additional burden.**
> Consequently, unbranded petrol and diesel will attract basic excise duty of **Rs 1.4, and
> Rs 1.8 per litre** respectively. The SAED on unbranded petrol and diesel shall be **Rs 11 and
> Rs 8 per litre** respectively. Similar changes have also been made for branded petrol and diesel."

And Part C of the same annexure, *"Imposition of Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess on
specified goods [**w.e.f. 2.2.2021**]"*, section (B) *On excise side*:

> "An agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess (AIDC) of **₹ 2.5 per litre** has been
> imposed on **petrol** and **₹ 4 per litre** on **diesel**. For other duties and cess, as revised,
> consequent to imposition of AIDC on these items refer to part B. **Overall there would be no
> additional burden on the consumer.**"

Section (A), *On customs side*, gives the customs limb, again with the same closing sentence —
*"For basic customs duty rates on these items refer to part B. **Overall there would be no
additional burden on the consumer on most of these items.**"*:

| item | proposed AIDC (customs) | | item | proposed AIDC (customs) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Gold, silver and dore bars | 2.5 % | | Peas | 40 % |
| Alcoholic beverages (ch. 22) | 100 % | | Kabuli chana | 30 % |
| Crude palm oil | 17.5 % | | Bengal gram / chick peas | 50 % |
| Crude soyabean and sunflower oil | 20 % | | Lentil (mosur) | 20 % |
| Apples | 35 % | | Cotton (not carded or combed) | 5 % |
| Coal, lignite and peat | 1.5 % | | Specified fertilizers (urea etc.) | 5 % |

The Basic Customs Duty side of the swap is on the face of the same annexure: the tariff table at
Part B carries the reduced BCD rates for exactly these items with the footnote *"refer to part C
for Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Cess rates on these items"* — e.g. ammonium nitrate
at 2.5 %, and *"Peas, kabuli chana, Bengal gram, lentils"* at **10 %** basic customs duty against
AIDC of 40 / 30 / 50 / 20 per cent on the same four items. **The basic rate on pulses was set at
10 per cent and the cess on the same pulses at up to 50 per cent.**

**The finding, stated precisely.** The Union did not merely *happen* to raise a cess while cutting
a basic rate. **It stated, three times in one annexure, that the cut was consequential on the cess
and was calibrated so that the consumer paid the same.** The proposition this part was asked to
establish is therefore established **from the Union's own budget document, in the Union's own
words**: the AIDC was, by design, **revenue-neutral to the taxpayer and not to the states.**

**Why this is the clearest single instance of the mechanism in the whole phase.**

- **Nothing about the taxpayer's position changed.** The Union says so in terms.
- **Everything about the states' position changed.** Basic excise duty and basic customs duty are
  shareable — the CAG certifies basic excise duty and nothing else as the net proceeds of the head
  (LR-3, F3). The AIDC is not. **Every rupee moved from BED/SAED/BCD to AIDC is a rupee that left
  the divisible pool without any taxpayer paying more and without any legislature voting on
  devolution.**
- **The transaction requires no exception, no emergency and no justification.** Article 270(1)
  supplies no test it could fail. It is exactly what LR-1's "strongest case for the states"
  describes, performed openly and documented by the party performing it.
- **The states' 41 per cent applied to none of it.** At **₹75,455.07 crore in FY2024-25 — 1.99 per
  cent of gross tax revenue** — the states' foregone entitlement on the AIDC alone, at 41 per cent,
  is on the order of **₹31,000 crore in that year**, from an instrument four years old.
- **And the auditor records that its fund did not exist.** ₹1,51,092.71 crore attributed to the
  Agriculture Infrastructure and Development Fund for FY2018-19→FY2022-23 with the status
  *"Fund not created as yet"* (CAG Report No. 4 of 2025, Figure 3.3). **A cess whose "specific
  purposes" had no fund, whose imposition was calibrated to change nothing for the taxpayer, and
  whose only measurable effect was on the divisible pool.**

**What remains unestablished, and it is narrow.** The Budget Speech annexure states the **new**
BED and SAED rates (₹1.4 and ₹11 per litre on unbranded petrol; ₹1.8 and ₹8 on unbranded diesel)
but **not the rates immediately before 2 February 2021**, so the arithmetic of the offset cannot be
closed from this document alone — the "From" figures are in the Finance Bill 2021 and the
Department of Revenue notifications of 1–2 February 2021, **neither of which was retrieved**.
**Do not state a per-litre "cut of ₹X" from this part.** The *fact* of the offset and its
*purpose* are established verbatim; only its exact size per litre is not. A-7 is accordingly
narrowed, not closed.

### PR-B — "Collected" vs "credited to the Fund": the CAG's third number

**The quantity.** How much cess money reached the purpose the cess was levied for.

**The competing definitions.** The **Union's** is *collections under the receipt head* — published
in every Receipt Budget, and the number all the shares in PR-A are built on. The **fund's** is
*credits to the reserve fund in the Public Account* — published in the Union Government Finance
Accounts. **Neither party publishes the difference.** The **CAG's** number is the difference, and
it exists only because the auditor subtracts:

- FY2018-19, all cesses: ₹2,74,592 cr collected, ₹1,64,322 cr transferred (Report No. 4 of 2020,
  para 2.5).
- GST Compensation Cess FY2017-18 + FY2018-19: **₹47,272 cr short-credited** (para 2.5.1).
- Health and Education Cess FY2018-19→FY2021-22: **MUSK nil, PMSSN nil, four and five years
  running** (Report No. 16 of 2025, Figure 3.6).
- R&D Cess FY1996-97→FY2016-17: ₹7,885.54 cr collected, **₹609.46 cr (7.73 %) disbursed**
  (Report No. 44 of 2017, para 2.3.1).
- Cess on crude oil: **₹2,94,150 cr retained in the CFI as at 31 March 2025** (Report No. 6 of
  2026, para 3.3.1).

**Definitional or evidentiary?** **Both, and the split is the point.** *Definitional* because
"collected" and "credited" are different accounting events on different funds and a source that
says "cess collections" is not lying when it means the first. *Evidentiary* because **whether the
credit actually occurred in a given year is a fact that only the CAG establishes**, and it
establishes it against the Union's interest, obtained an admission (February 2020) and a
correction (FY2019-20). **This is the one place in this part where a number exists that no party to
the dispute produced.**

### PR-D — Union excise duty: 11.1 per cent shareable, and three published documents needed to see it

**The quantity.** The share of Union excise duty entering the divisible pool, FY2023-24.

**What each instrument shows on its own.** The Receipt Budget's tax-revenue statement shows gross
Union excise duty of **₹3,05,362.18 crore** and, twenty lines apart in the same table, basic excise
duty of **₹33,786.92 crore** — but never subtracts them or comments. The CAG's certificate shows
net proceeds of Union excise duties of **₹33,820.71 crore** — but does not say what was excluded.
**Neither document states the ratio. The ratio is 11.1 per cent and it appears nowhere.**

**Definitional or evidentiary?** **Definitional**, and unusually clean: the two documents agree to
₹34 crore once the constitutional definition is applied. **This is the part's best example of a
finding that is fully supported by published Union sources and stated by none of them.** It is
also the reason CS-5 rather than CS-4 should carry the weight in stage 3: an itemised sum of levies
called cesses can be argued with; a certified residual cannot.

## Absences

### A-1 — **NOT PUBLISHED: the CAG's Article 279 net-proceeds certificate, for every year before FY2023-24 and by the CAG at all**

**The proposition searched for:** *"a document issued by the Comptroller and Auditor-General
certifying the net proceeds of Union taxes and duties under Article 279(1), for any year."*

**What was swept, and the positive control.** The `cag.gov.in` audit-report index was swept, and
the Regulations on Audit and Accounts 2020 were retrieved in two versions and searched. **Positive
control: the sweep of `https://cag.gov.in/en/audit-report?gt=50&yrf=2020&yrt=2020` returned 17
Union reports for 2020 including "Report No.4 of 2020 – Accounts of the Union Government –
Financial Audit" (detail id 112154), which was then downloaded and read.** The index works.

**Result.** No document titled or containing "net proceeds" exists in it. The **Regulations on
Audit and Accounts 2020** contain **zero occurrences of "net proceeds", "279" or "proceeds"** —
Regulation 43(1) provides only that the CAG *"examines and certifies the Finance Accounts and the
Appropriation Accounts."* **There is no regulation governing the Article 279 certificate.** And the
CAG's own Union Government Finance Accounts reports carry a footnote *reserving* the certificate:
*"subject to final ascertainment and certification under Article 279(1)."*

**The certificate's only published form is Annex 4C of the Union's own Receipt Budget, and it
exists for one year, FY2023-24**, because FC-16 recommended the disclosure only in its 2025 report
(para 7.30). Article 279(1) has required the certificate since 1950. **The number whose finality the
Constitution guarantees has been produced for seventy-six years and disclosed once, by the paying
party.** This is the single most consequential absence in the part; see §Disciplining measure.

### A-2 — **NOT PUBLISHED: the bridge between the Receipt Budget's gross tax revenue and the CAG's certified net proceeds**

**The proposition searched for:** *"a reconciliation showing, head by head, how gross tax revenue
becomes certified net proceeds — the deduction for cesses, for surcharges, for cost of collection,
and for anything else."*

Annex 4C gives the destination and the Receipt Budget's tax-revenue statement gives the origin.
**Nothing gives the road between them.** Two of the three large heads reconcile from published
data and one does not:

- **Union Excise Duties: reconciles to ~₹5 crore in 34 lakh.** 3,05,362.18 gross − 1,46,619.61
  SAED − 1,17,131.11 DoR cesses + 16.01 − 7,812.25 NCCD = 33,815.22, against certified
  **33,820.71**. (LR-3.)
- **Corporation Tax: reconciles to ~₹1,122 crore** (0.14 per cent, plausibly cost of collection).
  9,11,055.01 gross − 60,373.34 surcharge − 33,713.12 cess = 8,16,968.55 against certified
  8,15,846.17.
- **Customs: does NOT reconcile. ₹32,136.74 crore unexplained.** 2,33,118.97 gross − 16,273.41 SWS
  − 27,145.54 AIDC − 22.91 health cess + 3.19 export cesses − 2.99 RIC − 962.07 NCCD =
  1,88,715.24, against certified **1,56,578.50**. That is a **13.8 per cent** gap on the head, far
  too large for cost of collection, and no published document names what it is. (Duty-credit-scrip
  debits, the obvious candidate, are recorded at "…" — nil — for FY2023-24 in Receipt Budget
  2025-2026, so they are not the answer.)

**At the whole-tax-revenue level the residual is 1.27 percentage points of GTR, about ₹44,000
crore.** **Recorded as an unresolved discrepancy between two Union publications, not as an error in
either.** Stage 3 must not present the cess-and-surcharge decomposition as fully accounting for the
non-shareable wedge; it accounts for roughly 94 per cent of it.

### A-3 — **NEVER DEFINED: "cesses and surcharges" as an aggregate**

No Indian statute, budget document or Finance Commission report defines the set. Article 270(1)
excludes cesses and surcharges but supplies no list and no test. The Union's Receipt Budget does
not carry a "total cesses and surcharges" line — **it can be computed only by a reader selecting
line items**, which is why CS-2, CS-4, the Vidhi tables and the Union's own 6.7 per cent statement
to FC-16 are four different sums. D2 (a levy that is both) and D3 (a levy that is neither by name
but is by treatment) are the two places the absence of a definition does measurable damage.

### A-4 — **NOT PUBLISHED: the GDP base underlying the Finance Commissions' cess-to-GDP ratios**

FC-16 para 4.17 (non-shareable portion 1.1 per cent of GDP in FY2011-12 → 2.2 per cent in
FY2023-24) and FC-15 Table 4.1 do not state which GDP series they use. FY2011-12→FY2023-24 straddles
two of the instrument's three bases. **The ratios are therefore reported in CS-7 as those sources'
figures and are not proposed as a series.** The direction of the finding does not depend on the
base; the level does.

### A-5 — **NOT COLLECTED: no state-side measurement of Union cess collections exists, and none can**

The states do not administer Union cesses and have no assessment machinery for them. Every figure
the states put before FC-XV and FC-XVI is a Union figure. **This is a structural absence, not a
withholding**, and it means the phase's usual pairing — the Union's figure against the state's
figure, both quoted, neither adopted — **is unavailable for this part's central quantity.** It is
available for none of CS-2, CS-3, CS-4. Stage 3 must say so rather than leaving the reader to
assume the pairing was done.

### A-6 — **UNRETRIEVED: the Health Security se National Security Cess Act, 2025**

The Act is described and quantified in Receipt Budget 2026-2027 (LR-8) and was not separately
retrieved here. Its rate, its commencement date, its relationship to the expiring GST Compensation
Cess on the same goods, and whether any fund is designated for it are all unestablished.
**Marked unretrieved, not absent.**

### A-7 — **NARROWED, not closed: the pre-2 February 2021 excise rates on petrol and diesel**

**LR-9 is now established.** The Budget 2021-2022 Budget Speech annexure (S38) states verbatim that
Basic Excise Duty and Special Additional Excise Duty *"have been reduced … so that overall consumer

**What remains absent is only the "From" column**: the annexure does not state the rates in force
immediately before 2 February 2021, so the offset cannot be arithmetically closed from it. The
Finance Bill 2021 and the Department of Revenue notifications of 1–2 February 2021 were **not
retrieved.** **Consequence: stage 3 may assert the offset and its stated purpose, both verbatim,
but may not assert a per-litre cut of any particular size.**

## Definitional disagreements

### D1 — **Does "cesses and surcharges" include the GST Compensation Cess? — RESOLVED, and the answer is "it must be reported both ways"**

*This is the most consequential definitional choice in the part, and the phase asked for it to be
settled explicitly. Here is the settlement.*

**The legal fact is not in dispute.** The GST Compensation Cess is a cess. It is levied under the
GST (Compensation to States) Act 2017; it sits outside Article 270's divisible pool exactly as
every other cess does; and the CAG's Article 279 certificate for FY2023-24 (CS-5) names it as a
line and certifies its contribution to net proceeds as **a dash** — nil. On the legal test it is
unambiguously in.

**The economic fact points the other way and is equally undisputed.** Every rupee of the
compensation cess was, by statute, payable to the states — either as compensation under section 7
of the Compensation Act, or, after the borrowing arrangement, as service and repayment of the
back-to-back loans raised on the states' behalf. It never was Union revenue. **Counting it as
"revenue the states do not share" is counting a state entitlement as a state loss.**

**Therefore: including it inverts the finding, and the inversion is measurable.**

| | FY2011-12 | FY2017-18 | FY2018-19 | FY2020-21 (BE) | multiple |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **incl. comp. cess** (CS-2) | 10.4 | **13.9** | 19.9 | 19.9 | **×1.91** |
| **excl. comp. cess** (CS-3) | 10.4 | **10.6** | 15.3 | 15.3 | **×1.47** |

Two things to note. First, **in FY2017-18 the two series move in opposite directions** — the
incl-comp-cess measure rises from 13.5 to 13.9 while the excl measure *falls* from 13.5 to 10.6.
The single year most often used to date "the cess turn" is a year in which the cess burden on the
states *decreased* on the correct measure. Second, on my own FY2024-25 derivation (CS-4) the
difference is **13.94 vs 9.98 per cent — very nearly four percentage points, or 40 per cent of the
smaller figure.**

**The resolution adopted by this part.**

1. **The default convention is EXCLUDING the GST Compensation Cess.** A part asking "how much
   revenue do the states not share" must not count money that was contractually theirs.
2. **Both series are carried, with distinct ids** — CS-2 (incl.) and CS-3 (excl.). Neither is
   derived from the other by a note; they are two series.
3. **The exclusion has an end date.** The compensation cess ends; Receipt Budget 2026-2027 shows
   BE FY2026-27 at nil (LR-8). **From FY2026-27 the two conventions converge and the question
   dissolves** — but the cess it is being replaced by (Health Security se National Security Cess)
   is non-shareable in both senses, so the *level* does not fall by as much as the convergence
   suggests. Stage 3 must not read the convergence as relief.
4. **The convention each source uses, stated:**

| source | convention | evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **FC-XV Table 3.8** (S18) | **Includes**, but breaks the comp cess out as its own row and its own % of GTR — so it is *designed* to be netted | the table's own rows |
| **FC-XV para 4.25** (S18) | **Excludes** — states 13.1 % of GTR for FY2016-17→FY2018-19 | text |
| **FC-XV Table 4.1** (S18) | **Excludes** — "cesses and surcharges retained by the Union" as % of GDP | table title |
| **Vidhi study Annexure I-C** (S17) | **Excludes** (FY2012-13→FY2016-17 — necessarily; the cess did not exist) | source period |
| **Vidhi study Introduction** (S17) | **Includes** — the FY2017-18 RE and FY2018-19 BE figures list "GST Compensation Cess" at ₹61,331 cr / ₹90,000 cr among cesses in force at Annexure I-B | Annexure I-B item 6 |
| **The Union, to FC-16** (para 7.45, S21) | **Excludes** — *"Excluding GST compensation cess, cesses comprised 6.7 per cent of gross revenue…"* | verbatim |
| **CAG Article 279 certificate** (CS-5) | **Includes** as a named line, certified at nil | Annex 4C row 3 |
| **CAG Report No. 4 of 2020, para 2.5.1** | Treats it as a cess **and** as a state entitlement in the same paragraph: it audits the crediting of the cess to the Fund, and objects that booking it under "Transfer of Grants in aid to States" is wrong because *"GST Compensation Cess is the right of the States and is not a Grant in aid."* | verbatim |

**The Vidhi study is the trap case.** Its headline arc — 6.88 per cent of GTR in FY2012-13 rising
to **11.884 per cent** in FY2018-19 BE, from which it derives its proposed **ceiling** — is
**not a like-for-like series**: it switches from comp-cess-excluding to comp-cess-including at
FY2017-18, and it switches source from a CAG report to a Receipt Budget at the same join.
**A ceiling calibrated on a comp-cess-inclusive number and applied to a post-compensation world
is not the ceiling it was computed to be.** The study nowhere flags this. This is a first-class
definitional break and stage 3 must not quote the 11.884 figure as a like-for-like peak.

### D2 — **The same money is both a cess and a surcharge, and the two published series double-count it**

The Vidhi study's **footnote 502** discloses that the Health and Education Cess on income tax
*"is also included in the table providing details of Cess above in Annexure 1 because both the
nomenclature 'cess' and 'surcharge' is used."* The education, secondary-and-higher-education and
health-and-education cesses on income tax therefore appear in **both** the study's cess universe
(Annexure I) and its surcharge universe (Annexure II).

**Consequence:** the study's cess share (11.884 per cent, FY2018-19 BE) and surcharge share
(6.366 per cent) **may not be added.** The apparent leap in the surcharge share from **2.59 per
cent (FY2016-17) to 5.089 (FY2017-18) to 6.366 (FY2018-19)** is substantially this reclassification
rather than a change in what was levied. The study's own combined statement is confined to the one
year where the universes do not overlap in this way — FY2016-17, **₹2,16,704.6 crore**.

**Why this is not merely a bookkeeping quirk.** It reflects a real ambiguity in Article 270(1),
which excludes *both* "surcharge on taxes and duties referred to in article 271" *and* "any cess
levied for specific purposes". A levy styled a cess but computed as a percentage add-on to income
tax has the form of a surcharge and the name of a cess, and **nothing turns on which it is,
because both are excluded.** The Union has no incentive to disambiguate and no rule requiring it
to. The Vidhi study's own recommendation is that the government *"stop using 'cess' and 'surcharge'
interchangeably"* — a recommendation that is, on this evidence, addressed to a practice that makes
the aggregate unmeasurable rather than merely untidy.

### D3 — **Is the Special Additional Excise Duty a surcharge? The Union's own budget says yes and its own tables count it as neither**

The Receipt Budget 2026-2027's explanatory note to item 6.05 (S2), verbatim:

> "Special Additional Excise Duties (SAED) is leviable by the Finance Act, 2002 on Motor Spirit
> (Petrol) and High Speed Diesel Oil (HSD). **This is commonly known as surcharge.**"

**SAED is not styled a cess and not styled a surcharge in the statute** — it is an "additional duty
of excise". Yet:

- **The Union's own budget document calls it a surcharge**, in the note quoted.
- **The CAG's Article 279 certificate excludes it from net proceeds** — proved arithmetically at
  LR-3: certified Union excise net proceeds ₹33,820.71 crore against gross ₹3,05,362.18 crore,
  reconciling only if SAED is out.
- **Neither FC-XV's Table 3.8 nor the Vidhi cess table appears to carry it as such.** FC-XV's
  "indirect taxes (excl. GST)" row is ₹1,82,665 crore for FY2019-20 RE; SAED was small then
  (basic-excise restructuring came in FY2020-21) so the omission is invisible in that period.
  **But by FY2024-25 SAED is ₹1,42,880.01 crore, and whether it is in or out moves the cess-and-
  surcharge share of GTR by 3.76 percentage points.**

**The magnitude, from CS-4 (FY2024-25):**

| convention | % of GTR |
|---|---|
| excl. comp. cess, **excl.** SAED | **9.98** |
| excl. comp. cess, **incl.** SAED | **13.74** |
| incl. comp. cess, excl. SAED | 13.94 |
| incl. comp. cess, **incl.** SAED | **17.71** |

**Resolution.** SAED belongs **in** any measure whose question is *"how much of the Centre's tax
revenue is outside the divisible pool"*, because the CAG's certificate puts it outside and that is
final under Article 279(1). It belongs **out** of any measure whose question is *"how much revenue
is raised under levies styled cesses or surcharges"*, because it is styled neither. **These are
different questions and the phase should ask the first one.** The consequence is that the honest
headline figure for FY2024-25 is **13.74 per cent** (excl. comp. cess, incl. SAED) — a figure that
appears in no published source, because no published source asks the question that way.

**This is why CS-1 is the spine and not CS-2/3/4.** CS-1 measures the divisible pool directly and
is therefore immune to every one of D1, D2 and D3: it does not need to know whether SAED is a
surcharge or the health cess is a cess, because the CAG's certificate has already sorted every
rupee into the pool or out of it. **Where a definitional dispute cannot be resolved, measure the
residual instead of the components.**

### D4 — **Denominator: gross tax revenue or gross revenue receipts? The Union's 6.7 per cent and the states' 19.9 per cent are the same world**

The Union told FC-16 (para 7.45, S21): *"Excluding GST compensation cess, cesses comprised **6.7 per
cent of gross revenue** during the FC-15 period — less than the FC-13's 7.0 per cent."* The states
told the same Commission that the divisible pool had been eroded by *"excessive and rising cesses
and surcharges"* (FC-XV para 6.15).

**Four differences, every one of which moves the number the Union's way:**

1. **Denominator.** *Gross revenue* = gross tax revenue **plus non-tax revenue**. Non-tax revenue
   was ₹5,36,580 crore in FY2024-25 against GTR of ₹37,96,382 crore (S2, Annex 1) — a **14 per cent
   larger denominator.** Non-tax revenue is not shareable at all, so putting it in the denominator
   of a "share of revenue the states don't get" measure is a choice that can only reduce the ratio.
2. **Cesses only.** The Union's sentence says *"cesses"*, not "cesses and surcharges". Surcharges
   were ₹1,56,374.50 crore in FY2024-25 — **4.12 per cent of GTR on their own** (CS-4).
3. **Comp cess excluded.** Correct on this part's own default (D1) — but the Union excludes it here
   while the Union's budget documents include it in the cess universe elsewhere.
4. **Baseline chosen for comparison.** FC-XIII's period (FY2010-11→FY2014-15) is the period CS-1
   shows the divisible pool at 89.0–89.7 per cent of GTR, i.e. the period *before* the erosion.
   Comparing to it is comparing to the high-water mark of state entitlement.

**Are they disagreeing about facts?** **No. They are computing different ratios and both are
arithmetically correct.** There is no measurement in dispute anywhere in D4. This is the purest
instance in the part of the same facts weighted differently — and it is why **FC-16's own
adjudication (para 7.35) had to be about *directions* rather than levels**, and why its remedy
(para 7.30: publish the CAG certificate every year) is a **definitional** remedy. The Commission's
judgement, correctly, was that the parties needed a shared measure more than they needed a shared
number.

### D5 — Gross, net of refunds, credited to a fund, or certified: four events, four numbers

For a single cess in a single year there are at least four distinct quantities, and sources move
between them without notice:

| quantity | who publishes it | example, GST Compensation Cess FY2018-19 |
|---|---|---|
| **Gross collection** under the receipt head | Receipt Budget tax-revenue statement (S2–S15) | ₹95,081 crore |
| **Amount credited to the designated fund** | Union Government Finance Accounts, audited by the CAG | ₹54,275 crore |
| **The difference, retained in the Consolidated Fund** | **computed by the CAG and by nobody else** | **₹40,806 crore** |
| **Net proceeds under Article 279(1)** | CAG certificate, published only via Receipt Budget Annex 4C | nil, by construction |

**The third row is the whole of LR-4 and it exists only because an auditor performed a subtraction.**
It is also the reason "cess collections" and "cess spent on the cess's purpose" are not
interchangeable, and the reason the Union's defence in Report No. 21 of 2023 — that funds are
meant to be used *"over a period of years, but not necessarily, within each FY"* — is a defence
about the second row that does not answer the third.

**BE / RE / Actual.** Every series in this part states which it is, and several sources mix them
inside one table: FC-XV Table 3.8 is Actual to FY2018-19, **RE for FY2019-20 and BE for
FY2020-21**, in the same row; the Vidhi Introduction's headline 11.884 per cent is **BE**; FC-16
Figure 7.4 is CAG-certified Actual to FY2023-24 then **RE and BE**. **Three of the four most-quoted
numbers in this literature are budget estimates.** Any comparison that treats a BE as an outcome is
comparing an intention to a fact.

## Disciplining measure

**The question this part was asked to decide.** Part 01 found that on GST, the Council minute book
is a shared ledger of *arguments*, not of *facts*, because every number in it originates with the
Department of Revenue. The CAG's audit of the Union's own accounts is the strongest candidate in
the whole phase to be something better, because the auditor is neither payer nor payee. **Does the
part-01 reduction apply to the CAG, or is the CAG genuinely different?**

**The answer is: it depends on which of two different things the CAG is doing, and the two must be
separated, because they come to opposite conclusions.**

---

### The two CAG functions, and why they must not be conflated

**Function A — the Article 279(1) net-proceeds certificate.** The Constitution makes the CAG the
*measurer* of the divisible pool: net proceeds *"shall be ascertained and certified by the
Comptroller and Auditor-General of India, whose certificate shall be final."* This is not audit.
It is a constitutional adjudication of a quantity, with finality attached.

**Function B — the financial audit of the Accounts of the Union Government.** The CAG examines the
Union's accounts and reports what it finds — including that a cess was collected and not credited
to the fund it was levied for. This is audit in the ordinary sense.

---

### Function A fails the independence test — and the way it fails is the finding

**The CAG does not publish its Article 279 certificate.** The retrieving agent checked this three
independent ways on `cag.gov.in` and all three come back negative:

1. **No document.** No item titled or containing "net proceeds" appears in the CAG's audit-report
   index. The Combined Finance and Revenue Accounts (CAG-compiled) contain no net-proceeds
   statement.
2. **No regulation.** The **Regulations on Audit and Accounts 2020** — retrieved in both the
   approved text and the gazette notification — contain **zero occurrences of "net proceeds", "279"
   or "proceeds"**. Regulation 43(1) provides only that the CAG *"examines and certifies the
   Finance Accounts and the Appropriation Accounts."* **There is no regulation governing the
   Article 279 certificate at all.**
3. **The CAG's own reports disclaim the number.** Every Union Government Finance Accounts report
   read — No. 34 of 2016 p. 23, No. 44 of 2017, No. 7 of 2021 Table 1.4 — carries the identical
   footnote under the devolution table: *"The transfer to States in the form of share in central
   tax revenues as shown in Union Government Finance Accounts is **subject to final ascertainment
   and certification under Article 279(1)**."*

**Read (3) carefully, because it is the crux.** The CAG's published reports do not certify the
devolution figure. They **reproduce the Union's figure and expressly reserve the certificate for
later, elsewhere.** The certificate is performed, its result is real — Annex 4C shows it moved
₹623.47 crore of recovery from the states for FY2023-24 — but it is performed **out of public view
and disclosed only by the payer.**

**So the only published form of the Article 279 certificate in existence is Annex 4C of the Union's
own Receipt Budget.** The number is the auditor's. **The publication is the Union's.** No third
party can obtain the certificate from the CAG; the states must take the Union's transcription of
it. And it exists for exactly **one year, FY2023-24**, because the Sixteenth Finance Commission
only recommended the disclosure in its 2025 report.

**Verdict on Function A: the part-01 reduction applies, in a modified and slightly worse form.**
On GST, every number in the Council minute book originated with the Department of Revenue. Here,
the number originates with the CAG — that is better — but **the only channel to it is the
Department of Revenue's own budget document**, and there is no independent copy against which the
transcription can be checked. Part 01's finding was *"a shared ledger of arguments, not of facts."*
Function A gives a shared ledger of one fact, for one year, **published by one of the two
parties**. It is an improvement on the Council minute book and it is not evidentiary independence.

**Is that definitional discipline or evidentiary independence?** **Definitional discipline.** The
certificate does not re-collect anything. Article 279(1) defines net proceeds as *"the proceeds
thereof reduced by the cost of collection"* — the CAG takes the Union's proceeds figures and the
Union's cost-of-collection figures and applies the constitutional definition to them. What it
supplies is an **authoritative and final ruling on what counts**, which is exactly what a
definitional dispute needs and exactly what this part is otherwise full of (D1–D4). It supplies no
new observation of the world.

**And the discipline is real even so, which is why it must not be dismissed.** Test it: does the
certificate ever contradict the Union? **Yes, and Annex 4C records the contradiction.** For
FY2023-24 the CAG certified net proceeds of ₹27,69,957.40 crore, states' share at 41 per cent of
₹11,35,682.53 crore, against ₹11,36,306.00 crore actually released — and the Receipt Budget
2026-2027 accordingly books **"States' share Final settlement based on C&AG certified net proceeds:
−623.47"**, a recovery from the states. **A definitional ruling that moves cash against the party
publishing it is not nothing.** It is a genuine constraint on the *arithmetic* of the pool. It is
simply not a constraint on the *composition* of the pool, because the composition is set by
Parliament's choice of instrument, and the CAG certifies whatever composition Parliament produces.

### Function B passes, and is the strongest instrument in this phase

The cess-to-reserve-fund findings (LR-4) are a different animal, and the part-01 reduction does
**not** apply to them, for four reasons that can each be tested.

1. **The CAG derives the finding, it does not receive it.** No Union document states "₹47,272 crore
   of compensation cess was short-credited" or "MUSK received nil for five years". These are
   **differences the CAG computed** between two Union records — collections under a receipt head
   and credits to a fund in the Public Account — that the Union publishes separately and never
   subtracts. The inputs are the Union's; **the finding is not.** That is precisely the step the
   GST Council minute book never takes.
2. **It contradicts the Union and the Union has conceded.** The Ministry **accepted** the
   ₹47,272 crore finding in February 2020, and Report No. 7 of 2021 shows the behaviour changed —
   ₹24,945 crore transferred in excess in FY2019-20, and no shortfall in FY2020-21. **An instrument
   that produced an admission and a correction is doing something a shared record of positions
   cannot do.**
3. **It exposes a contradiction *between two Union sources* that neither would have disclosed.**
   Report No. 16 of 2025 records the Ministry of Finance claiming transfers of ₹3,66,152.31 crore
   against ₹2,64,632.07 crore in the Union Government Finance Accounts, and directs that
   *"[t]his needs to be reconciled by the Ministry."* **A ₹1.02 lakh crore discrepancy inside the
   Union's own books, surfaced by the auditor.** No party to the Centre–state dispute produced it
   and neither party benefits from its existence.
4. **It is longitudinal and adversarial to its own subject.** Report No. 44 of 2017 records that
   the Central Road Fund short-transfer had been reported in Reports 1 of 2013, 1 of 2014, 1 of
   2015, 50 of 2015 and 34 of 2016 — **six consecutive reports on the same uncorrected defect.**
   An instrument produced by a party to the dispute does not repeat an unwelcome finding six times.

**Verdict on Function B: genuine evidentiary independence, within a bounded scope.** The bound must
be stated plainly: **the CAG does not independently re-collect tax revenue.** It cannot tell you
whether the Union's stated collection of the Health and Education Cess was ₹41,309.07 crore or
something else; it takes that from the accounts. What it independently establishes is **what
happened to money once inside the Union's accounts** — and since the entire cess controversy is
about the *disposition* of money rather than its *amount*, that is the part that matters here.

### The trap, applied to this part

**Union-reported figures are not independent of the Centre.** Every collection figure in CS-2, CS-3
and CS-4 is the Department of Revenue's. FC-XV Table 3.8 says so on its face ("Union Budget
documents"). The Vidhi study's cess table is sourced to a CAG report — which is itself reporting
the Union's collections. **Three sources, one origin.** The apparent triangulation in this part is
largely illusory and stage 3 must not present it as corroboration. The one place it is *not*
illusory is CS-5 vs CS-1, where a CAG certificate and a Finance Commission figure agree to 79.93 vs
79.9 — and even there, the Commission says its source *is* the CAG certificates, so the agreement
confirms transcription, not measurement.

**State-reported figures are not independent of the states.** This part contains none, and that is
itself worth recording: **no state publishes, or could publish, an independent measurement of Union
cess collections.** The states' entire case before FC-XV and FC-XVI (paras 6.15, 7.36–7.39) is
argued from Union figures. The asymmetry is structural — the party with the grievance has no
instrument of its own — and it is the reason the CAG's Function B matters so much more than its
share of this part's word count would suggest.

**The one thing to carry forward.** Part 01's reduction was that a shared record can be a ledger of
arguments while every fact in it comes from one party. This part shows the reduction has a **limit**:
an auditor who *subtracts two of the payer's own numbers and publishes the difference* has produced
a fact neither party supplied. That is a narrow gate, but the CAG walks through it ten times in ten
years, and no other instrument in this phase does.
