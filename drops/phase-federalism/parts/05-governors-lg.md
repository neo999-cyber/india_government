# Part 05 — Governors and the office of the Lieutenant Governor

STATUS: IN PROGRESS (written incrementally; sections appended as evidence lands)

## Findings

**The eleven things this part establishes, each with the instrument that establishes it.**

**F-1. The dispute is about a silence, and both sides read the same silence.** Articles 200 and 201
fix **no period** for the Governor or the President. The one hard number in the scheme — **six
months** — binds the **State legislature**. The Constitution contains deeming provisions in Articles
107, 108, 109(4)–(5), 196, 197, 198(4)–(5), 213 and 239B and **none** in 200 or 201. *(Constitution of
India, 1 May 2024 edition, pages 121–122, read directly; the deeming list from 2025 INSC 1333 para
117.)*

**F-2. On 8 April 2025 a two-judge Bench read timelines into Articles 200 and 201 and, for the first
time, declared ten State bills deemed assented under Article 142.** *(2025 INSC 481, conclusions XIV,
XIX and paragraph 435(c) — the Supreme Court's own 415-page PDF.)*

**F-3. On 20 November 2025 a five-judge Bench, on a Presidential Reference of 13 May 2025, held that
timelines may NOT be judicially prescribed, that the Governor is NOT bound by ministerial advice
under Article 200, that his function is NOT justiciable on the merits, and that *"the Constitution,
specifically Article 142 even, does not allow for the concept of 'deemed assent' of Bills."***
*(2025 INSC 1333, opinions 165.2, 165.3, 165.5, 165.7, 165.10 — the Court's own 111-page PDF, read
directly.)* **What survives for the States is a limited mandamus for inaction that is *"prolonged,
unexplained, and indefinite"* (165.3), and the holding that Article 361 immunises the Governor's
person but not his office (165.4).**

**F-4. The Tamil Nadu relief was expressly NOT vacated.** *"The questions do not require this Court
to vacate, amend, or modify the final relief granted by this Court in State of Tamil Nadu"* (2025
INSC 1333 para 26). **Ten bills remain deemed assented as of 18 November 2023 under a doctrine that
no longer exists. Report both halves or report it wrong.**

**F-5. There is no national count of bills pending with Governors.** Neither 2025 judgment cites one.
**A Constitution Bench decided the legal effect of a quantity that nobody put a national figure on.**
The only aggregate anywhere is **"185 Bills"**, asserted by the Punjab Governor's counsel with no
period and no denominator, which the Court held irrelevant rather than verifying (2023 INSC 1017
paras 13(iii), 27). *(For the parliamentary-corpus side of this, see §9.)*

**F-6. What does exist is bill-level, dated, two-sided — and only for states that sued.** Tamil Nadu:
a **twelve-row table with four dates each**, ten bills over a year with the Governor, two over three
years, **all twelve acted on the same day, three days after *State of Punjab*.** Kerala: **eight
bills, seven to twenty-three months**, seven reserved for the President **eight days after the Court
issued notice**, on which the Court found *"No reason or justification has been adduced."* Punjab:
four bills plus three Money Bills, with two Money Bills recommended *"after the institution of the
Petition."* **In all three, the litigation is what ended the pendency. The observation instrument is
the writ petition.**

**F-7. Delhi: a Constitution Bench holding of 11 May 2023 was reversed by statute with effect from
19 May 2023 — eight days — and Article 239AA(7)(b) expressly permits exactly that.** Part IV-A of the
GNCTD Act creates the National Capital Civil Service Authority (Chief Minister as Chairperson, Chief
Secretary *"appointed by the Central Government"*, Principal Home Secretary; majority vote; quorum
two), makes it a matter on which the LG acts *"in his sole discretion"*, and provides that *"in case
of difference of opinion, the decision of the Lieutenant Governor shall be final."* *(Consolidated
GNCTD Act 1991 as on 15 December 2025, Act 19 of 2023, every date from the Act's own footnotes.)*
Act 15 of 2021 had already provided that *"'Government' … shall mean the Lieutenant Governor."*

**F-8. Delhi and Puducherry each have a statutory power on assent that no State's Governor has.**
GNCTD s.24 and Government of Union Territories Act 1963 s.25 both allow the LG/Administrator to
**reserve a re-passed Bill for the President** — the exact act held illegal for the Governor of Tamil
Nadu — and each carries **four** mandatory-reservation triggers against Article 200's one. **And both
territories vote as States in the GST Council under Article 366(26B)** (part 01).

**F-9. The chancellor dispute is the assent dispute.** **Twelve of twelve** Tamil Nadu bills, five of
the named Kerala bills and two of four Punjab bills were university Acts. **The Governor is
Chancellor under State statute; removing him needs a State Bill; that Bill needs his assent.** In
West Bengal the same conflict produced ~35 universities without regular Vice-Chancellors, 24
appointments quashed, 28 interim VCs appointed by the Chancellor, the **Attorney General appearing
for the Chancellor**, and finally the Supreme Court constituting Search-cum-Selection Committees
itself **under Article 142** *(2024 INSC 469, the Court's own PDF)* — the same power the November 2025
opinion says cannot supply a Governor's assent.

**F-10. The Union's own Ministry of Home Affairs fixed a three-month limit on Article 201 disposal in
2016 because it found *"undue delay"* in its own system — and the text is public only because
litigation printed it.** Two office memoranda of **4 February 2016**, File No. 23/18/2015-Judl & PP
(Part III), reproduced **in full** at 2025 INSC 481 paragraphs 385 and 387. **No independent
government publication of either was found.** The Cabinet-approved guidelines they build on (D.O.
23/33/1992-Judl of 22 September 1992) remain unretrieved.

**F-11. The reports of both Commissions on Centre–State Relations are currently unreadable, and the
Union's stated reason for not implementing them is that the process is unfinished — for sixteen
years.** `interstatecouncil.gov.in` resolves on six resolvers but its port 443 is closed and no
Wayback capture of any chapter PDF returns 200. **Sarkaria and Punchhi survive in this part only as
quoted by the NCRWC report and the Supreme Court.** NCRWC itself recommended appointment and removal
of Governors *"after consultation with the Chief Minister"* and **six months / three months** on
assent. Punchhi's 273 recommendations went to the **11th Inter-State Council on 16 July 2016**, then
to a Standing Committee that met three times in 2017–18, then to a decision *"to obtain updated
comments"* — and **the Inter-State Council has met eleven times in thirty-six years and not once
since 2016** *(MHA Annual Reports 2016-17 paras 4.6–4.9 and 2024-25 paras 17.5, 17.7).*

**The single sentence, if only one survives.** *The Constitution binds the elected legislature to a
clock and the Union's appointee to none; the Supreme Court supplied a clock in April 2025 and a
larger Bench removed it in November 2025; and the quantity the whole dispute is about — how many
bills are pending, and for how long — is measured by nobody, so that it becomes visible only in the
states that sue, only at the moment they sue, and never anywhere else.*

### §1 — Articles 200 and 201: the text, and what the text does not contain

**Instrument retrieved (T1).** *The Constitution of India (As on 1st May, 2024)*, Government of
India, Ministry of Law and Justice, Legislative Department, Official Languages Wing, 402 pages.
Downloaded as PDF from `https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s380537a945c7aaa788ccfcdf1b99b5d8f/uploads/2024/07/20240716890312078.pdf`
(the Legislative Department's S3WAAS content-delivery host; `legislative.gov.in` itself would not
complete a TLS handshake from this environment — see §Sources NOT retrieved). Articles 199(4), 200,
201 and 202 are on PDF pages 121–122, printed pages 91–92, Part VI (The States), Chapter III.
Text below is transcribed from that PDF.

**Article 200, substantive part — verbatim:**

> **200. Assent to Bills.**—When a Bill has been passed by the Legislative Assembly of a State or,
> in the case of a State having a Legislative Council, has been passed by both Houses of the
> Legislature of the State, it shall be presented to the Governor and the Governor shall declare
> either that he assents to the Bill or that he withholds assent therefrom or that he reserves the
> Bill for the consideration of the President:

**First proviso — verbatim:**

> Provided that the Governor may, **as soon as possible** after the presentation to him of the Bill
> for assent, return the Bill if it is not a Money Bill together with a message requesting that the
> House or Houses will reconsider the Bill or any specified provisions thereof and, in particular,
> will consider the desirability of introducing any such amendments as he may recommend in his
> message and, when a Bill is so returned, the House or Houses shall reconsider the Bill
> accordingly, and if the Bill is passed again by the House or Houses with or without amendment and
> presented to the Governor for assent, **the Governor shall not withhold assent therefrom**:

**Second proviso — verbatim:**

> Provided further that the Governor shall not assent to, but shall reserve for the consideration of
> the President, any Bill which in the opinion of the Governor would, if it became law, so derogate
> from the powers of the High Court as to endanger the position which that Court is by this
> Constitution designed to fill.

**Article 201 — verbatim:**

> **201. Bills reserved for consideration.**—When a Bill is reserved by a Governor for the
> consideration of the President, the President shall declare either that he assents to the Bill or
> that he withholds assent therefrom:
>
> Provided that, where the Bill is not a Money Bill, the President may direct the Governor to return
> the Bill to the House or, as the case may be, the Houses of the Legislature of the State together
> with such a message as is mentioned in the first proviso to article 200 and, when a Bill is so
> returned, the House or Houses shall reconsider it **within a period of six months from the date of
> receipt of such message** and, if it is again passed by the House or Houses with or without
> amendment, it shall be presented again to the President for his consideration.

**What the text does NOT contain — and this asymmetry is the whole dispute.**

1. **No period is fixed for the Governor.** The only temporal expression binding the Governor is the
   first proviso's *"as soon as possible"*, and it is attached to the act of *returning* a Bill, not
   to the act of *deciding*. The substantive part of Article 200 — the part that carries the three
   options — has no time expression of any kind.
2. **No period is fixed for the President under Article 201.** The substantive part is bare.
3. **The one hard number in the whole of Articles 200–201 runs against the State legislature, not
   against the Union's appointee.** It is the *"six months"* in the proviso to Article 201, which
   binds the *House or Houses* to reconsider a returned Bill. The Constitution imposes a deadline on
   the elected legislature and none on the Governor or the President. That is a textual fact and
   both sides in the litigation accept it; what they disagree about is what follows from it.
4. **No consequence is stated for inaction.** Articles 200 and 201 provide no deeming provision, no
   lapse, no default assent. Contrast Article 111 (Union Bills) — also silent — and contrast
   Articles 198(5) and 199, where the Constitution *does* deem things passed after fourteen days
   when it wants to. **The framers knew how to write a deeming clause and did not write one into
   Article 200.** This is the strongest textual point available to the Union's side, and it is
   the point on which the April 2025 judgment had to rely on Article 142 rather than on Article 200.

**The `as soon as possible` construction is itself a definitional break.** In the litigation the
phrase does two opposite jobs. On the States' reading it colours the whole of Article 200 with
urgency (this is what the Supreme Court held in April 2025, conclusion XI). On the Union's reading
it is a proviso-local qualifier attaching only to return-with-message, so that a Governor who has
not yet chosen among the three options is not within its reach at all. **These are two readings of
the same sixteen words; they are not two sets of facts.** Note this for §Disciplining measure.

### §2 — The assent litigation, in order

#### 2.1 State of Punjab v Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab — 10 November 2023 — **RETRIEVED, SUPREME COURT'S OWN PDF**

`https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2023/44896/44896_2023_1_19_48220_Judgement_10-Nov-2023.pdf`
— **27 pages**, 335,149 bytes, same `--resolve` treatment. Caption page 1: `**2023 INSC 1017** /
Reportable / IN THE SUPREME COURT OF INDIA / CIVIL ORIGINAL JURISDICTION / **Writ Petition (Civil)
No 1224 of 2023** / State of Punjab …Petitioner / Versus / Principal Secretary to the Governor of
Punjab and Another …Respondents`. Signed page 27 by **Dr Dhananjaya Y Chandrachud CJI, J B
Pardiwala and Manoj Misra JJ**, `New Delhi; November 10, 2023`. *(Reported at (2024) 1 SCC 384 —
that citation is the one the April 2025 judgment uses.)*

**The facts, paragraphs 4–10, pages 4–7, read directly.** The Speaker adjourned the Sixteenth Punjab
Vidhan Sabha *sine die* on **22 March 2023**, reconvened it on **19 and 20 June 2023**, and it passed
four Bills: the Sikh Gurdwaras (Amendment) Bill 2023; Punjab Affiliated Colleges (Security of
Service) (Amendment) Bill 2023; Punjab Universities Law (Amendment) Bill 2023; Punjab Police
(Amendment) Bill 2023. *"No action was taken by the Governor on these Bills."* Three Money Bills
followed for the **20 October 2023** sitting. The Governor's letters of **17 July**, **24 July**,
**12 October** and **19 October 2023** said the sessions were *"patently (sic) illegal"* on legal
advice from *"a constitutional expert"*, and concluded: *"For these reasons **I withhold my approval
to the above mentioned Bills**."* Paragraph 8: *"**Notably, the Governor did not 'declare' in any
public notification that he is withholding his assent to the Bills.**"* The State sought a
declaration that the sessions were valid and a mandamus that **seven Bills** kept pending be
processed. Petition entertained **6 November 2023**.

**The holding on the first proviso, paragraphs 20–25, pages 13–17, read directly.** The first proviso
*"does not qualify the first option … nor the third option … [it] attaches to the second option
(withholding of assent)"*. Paragraph 24: *"if the Governor decides to withhold assent … the Governor
**must mandatorily follow the course of action which is indicated in the first proviso** … The
expression 'as soon as possible' is significant. It conveys a **constitutional imperative of
expedition**. Failure to take a call and keeping a Bill duly passed for indeterminate periods is a
course of action inconsistent with that expression. Constitutional language is not surplusage."*
Paragraph 25: without that reading *"the Governor as the unelected Head of State would be in a
position to **virtually veto** the functioning of the legislative domain by a duly elected
legislature by simply declaring that assent is withheld without any further recourse."*
Paragraph 44: *"**Casting doubt on the validity of the session of the House is not a constitutional
option open to the Governor.**"* Paragraph 45: *"we have not expressed any opinion in regard to the
manner in which the Governor will exercise his jurisdiction on the Bills."*

**And here is the Union appointee's counter-statistic, which is the phase's cleanest example of the
trap in §(c) of my brief.** Paragraph 13(iii), page 9, recording senior counsel for the Principal
Secretary to the Governor: *"**The Governor has, as a matter of fact, assented to as many as 185
Bills** which were presented to him for assent, which would clearly indicate that there has been no
delay on the part of the Governor and it is only in view of the objection to the manner in which the
House was adjourned sine die that assent to the four Bills was withheld."* The Court's response,
paragraph 27, page 18: *"these submissions do not affect the role of the Governor under the
Constitution or justify the inaction on the Bills."*

**Read that exchange carefully, because it is the disciplining-measure question in miniature.**
The figure **185** is on a Supreme Court record. It is the *only* aggregate assent count I found
anywhere in this research. But it was **supplied by the Governor's counsel, over no stated period,
against no stated denominator, and the Court neither verified it nor rejected it — it held it
irrelevant.** A number on a judgment's record is not thereby a found fact. **185 must not be
authored as a data point.** It is a party's assertion, recorded. This is the same shape part 01
found in the GST Council minute book: a shared ledger of *arguments*, not of *facts*.

**What the Court held — from the Conclusion, paragraphs 434–435, PDF pages 387–409, printed pages

| # | Holding, as stated by the Court |
|---|---|
| I | Article 200 gives the Governor **three** options: assent, withhold assent, reserve for the President |
| II | The first proviso is **not an independent course of action**; it must be initiated whenever assent is withheld. *State of Punjab* is correct |
| V | *"Neither the concept of 'pocket veto' nor that of 'absolute veto' finds place within the constitutional scheme"*; `shall declare` means **there is no scope of inaction**; *"as soon as possible" … does not allow the Governor to sit on the bills* |
| VII | As a general rule the Governor **may not reserve a bill for the President in the second round**, after it has been returned and re-passed — the only exception being a bill *"materially different"* from the first (para 204) |
| VIII | On these facts the reservation of the **ten Bills** in the second round was *"illegal, erroneous in law"* and is set aside; **any subsequent action taken upon the said Bills by the President also does not survive and is thus set aside** |
| IX | The Bills *"are **deemed to have been assented to** by the Governor on the date when they were presented to him after being reconsidered"* |
| X | **"There is no expressly specified time-limit for the discharge of the functions by the Governor under Article 200."** The Court says this in terms — it does not pretend the text contains one |
| XII–XIII | Where no time-limit is prescribed a power must be exercised in reasonable time (*A.G. Perarivalan*, *Keisham*); prescribing one is *"not the same thing as amending the text of the Constitution"* |
| XIV | **The timelines, prescribed by the Court.** Non-compliance makes the inaction judicially reviewable |
| XV | The Governor under Article 200 must **generally act on the aid and advice of the Council of Ministers**; the exceptions are the second proviso and Article 163(1) |
| XVI | *B.K. Pavitra* is declared **per incuriam** on two points: that the Governor has discretion on reservation, and that his Article 200 discretion is beyond judicial scrutiny. Reason given: the words *"in his discretion"* present in **section 75 of the Government of India Act 1935** were **removed** when it was adapted into Article 200 |
| XVIII | **There is no pocket veto or absolute veto under Article 201 either**; the President must choose, and withholding must be accompanied by reasons |
| XIX | **The President must decide within three months** of receiving the reference; delay beyond that requires recorded reasons conveyed to the State. The Court says it is *"having regard to the reports of Sarkaria and Puncchi Commissions, as well as the **Memorandum dated 04.02.2016 issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs**"* |
| XX | Where a bill is reserved on grounds of patent unconstitutionality imperilling representative democracy, *"as a measure of prudence, the President **ought to make a reference to this Court in exercise of his powers under Article 143**"* |
| XXIV | Full taxonomy of when gubernatorial and presidential action is justiciable |

**The timelines the Court read in — conclusion (XIV), verbatim, printed page 393:**

| situation | limit prescribed |
|---|---|
| withholding assent **or** reserving for the President, **on the advice** of the State Council of Ministers | *"forthwith, subject to a maximum period of **one-month**"* |
| withholding assent **contrary to** the advice of the Council of Ministers | return the bill with a message within a maximum of **three months** |
| reserving for the President **contrary to** the advice of the Council of Ministers | reserve within a maximum of **three months** |
| a bill presented **after reconsideration** under the first proviso | assent *"forthwith, subject to a maximum period of **one-month**"* |
| the **President** under Article 201 (conclusion XIX) | **three months** from receipt of the reference |

**The Article 142 step — paragraph 435(c), printed page 409, verbatim:**

> *"Having regard to the unduly long period of time for which these Bills were kept pending by the
> Governor before the ultimate declaration of withholding of assent and in view of the scant respect
> shown by the Governor to the decision of this Court in State of Punjab (supra) and other
> extraneous considerations that appear to be writ large in the discharge of his functions, we are
> left with no other option but to exercise our inherent powers under Article 142 of the
> Constitution for the purpose of declaring these ten Bills as deemed to have been assented on the
> date when they were presented to the Governor after being reconsidered by the State legislature
> i.e., on 18.11.2023."*

**Note the structure of that sentence, because it is the pivot of the whole dispute.** The Court did
**not** hold that Article 200 contains a deeming provision. It held (X) that it does not, then used
**Article 142** — the Court's own plenary power to do complete justice — to supply the effect the
text withholds. That distinction is what the Presidential Reference then attacked.

**So, on the two questions the brief asks me to settle, plainly:**

1. **Can timelines be read into Articles 200 and 201? — NO.** Opinion 165.5 and 165.7. The Court's
   reasoning is at paragraphs 112–115: *Keisham* concerned the Speaker's **quasi-judicial** function
   under the Tenth Schedule and *"cannot be equated with the gubernatorial function"*; *State of
   Telangana* held only that *"as soon as possible"* carries *"significant constitutional content"*
   and *"offers no assistance"*; *Durga Pada Ghosh* on *"as soon as may be"* in Article 22(5) is a
   habeas corpus case. Paragraph 114 is explicit: *"paragraphs 260-261 of the judgment in State of
   Tamil Nadu (supra), pertaining to the imposition of timelines on the Governor under Article 200
   **are erroneous**"* (with corresponding conclusions 456.10 to 456.14.4), and the Article 201
   timeline observations *"are merely **obiter**, and ought to be treated as such."*
   Paragraph 115: *"The text of Articles 200 and 201, has been framed in such a manner, so as to
   provide a sense of **elasticity** … The imposition of timelines would be strictly contrary to
   this elasticity that the Constitution so carefully preserves."*
2. **Does deemed assent survive? — NO, as a proposition of law.** Opinion 165.10. Paragraph 117 is
   the textual argument and it is the strongest thing in the opinion: *"the Constitution framers were
   categorical as to when deeming provisions were to operate … Article 109(4) and 109(5) … Article
   198(4) and (5) … Articles 107, 108, 196, 197, 213 and 239B also contemplate a form of deeming, as
   a consequence. **Such deeming clauses are absent in the case of Articles 200 and 201**, therefore,
   we find that by judicial intervention such assent cannot be deemed."* Paragraph 118: deemed assent
   is *"virtually a takeover of the role, and function, of a separate constitutional authority"* and
   offends separation of powers, part of the basic structure. Paragraph 119: the TN Court
   *"does not employ Article 142 to merely fill the gaps … but a substantive one which finds no basis
   in the text of the Constitution."*

**AND THE PART THAT MATTERS MOST FOR THIS INSTRUMENT — the two holdings are not co-extensive in
time or in persons, and anybody who reports one without the other will report it wrong.**

- The **general propositions** of the April 2025 judgment — timelines, deemed assent, aid-and-advice
  binding the Governor, merits justiciability — are **repudiated for the future, for all States**.
- The **operative relief in the Tamil Nadu case was expressly NOT vacated.** Paragraph 26, page 21:
  *"The questions do not require this Court to vacate, amend, or modify the final relief granted by
  this Court in State of Tamil Nadu (supra)"*; paragraph 27: *"'sitting in appeal' would mean the
  variation, or vacation of the operative order in a concluded lis. … Article 143 cannot be invoked
  to overturn a concluded adjudication inter-se parties."*
- **Therefore: the ten Tamil Nadu bills remain deemed assented as of 18 November 2023 as a matter of
  inter-partes relief, while the doctrine that produced that relief has been held to be unavailable.**
  This is a real and unusual state of affairs and it must be recorded as such, not smoothed over. It
  is also the reason a "deemed assent" count can never become a series: **n = 10, and the mechanism
  that produced it has been closed.** Part 06 (Tamil Nadu) needs this.
- **What survives on the States' side is narrow but real:** 165.3's *"limited mandamus"* for
  *"prolonged, unexplained, and indefinite"* inaction, and 165.4's holding that Article 361 does not
  shield the **office** even though it shields the **person**. A State can still get an order
  directing the Governor to decide. It can no longer get a date by which he must, nor a court-supplied
  assent if he does not.

**One instrument surfaces here that matters for §4 and for the Absences.** Paragraph 113, page 76:
*"The Court further relied on **Circulars issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs**, to hold that
there were applicable timelines on both the Governor under Article 200, and the President under
Article 201. We are of the considered view that the Ministry's circulars **cannot be incorporated by
way of judicial interpretation** in such a manner, so as to apply as a constitutional fetter."* Read

### §4 — Article 239AA and the Lieutenant Governor in Delhi

**The constitutional text (T1, from the same Constitution PDF, pages 143–146, printed pages 113–116).**
Articles 239AA and 239AB were *"ins. by the Constitution (Sixty-ninth Amendment) Act, 1991, s. 2
(w.e.f. 1-2-1992)"*. The load-bearing clauses:

- **239AA(3)(a)** — the Legislative Assembly may legislate on State List and Concurrent List matters
  *"except matters with respect to **Entries 1, 2 and 18 of the State List** and Entries 64, 65 and
  66 of that List in so far as they relate to the said Entries 1, 2 and 18."* (Entries 1, 2, 18 =
  public order, police, land.)
- **239AA(4)** — a Council of Ministers *"to aid and advise the Lieutenant Governor in the exercise
  of his functions in relation to matters with respect to which the Legislative Assembly has power
  to make laws, **except in so far as he is, by or under any law, required to act in his
  discretion**"*, with the proviso that *"in the case of difference of opinion between the
  Lieutenant Governor and his Ministers on any matter, the Lieutenant Governor shall **refer it to
  the President** for decision and act according to the decision given thereon by the President"* —
  and, pending that decision, may act unilaterally if the matter is *"so urgent"*.
- **239AA(7)(a)** — Parliament may by law *"make provisions for giving effect to, or supplementing"*
  these clauses; **(7)(b)** — and *"Any such law … **shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this
  Constitution for the purposes of article 368** notwithstanding that it contains any provision
  which amends or has the effect of amending, this Constitution."*
- **239AB** — the President may suspend the operation of any provision of Article 239AA if
  *"a situation has arisen in which the administration of the National Capital Territory cannot be
  carried on"* **or** — a second, much wider limb — *"that for the proper administration of the
  National Capital Territory it is necessary or expedient so to do."*

**239AA(7)(b) is the mechanism at the heart of the 2021 and 2023 amendments, and it is doing exactly
what its words say.** Parliament may pass a law that has the effect of amending the Constitution's
Delhi provisions, by simple majority, without the Article 368 machinery. The Union's statutory
answers to the Supreme Court in 2021 and 2023 rest on it. **This is a case where the Union's power
is textual and express, and the states'-rights reading has to argue around the text, not with it.**

makes transfers and postings of Group 'A' and DANICS officers a matter on which the LG acts *"in his
sole discretion"* (s.41(1)(iii)) and in which *"the decision of the Lieutenant Governor shall be
final"* (s.45H(3), third proviso). **That the operative date of a statute is eight days after a
Constitution Bench judgment on the same subject is a fact on the face of the Legislative
Department's own consolidated Act, and it needs no characterisation to be significant.**

**On the Ordinance.** The brief asks me to establish the sequence exactly. I did **not** retrieve
the Ordinance from the Gazette (see §Sources NOT retrieved — `egazette.gov.in` returned HTTP 404 on

the Ordinance.** What is retrieved at T1 is that **Part IV-A has legal effect from 19 May 2023**.

#### 4.4 Delhi's assent provision is NOT Article 200, and the difference runs the Union's way

**This is a definitional break the assent debate does not price, and it is visible only by reading
section 24 of the GNCTD Act against Article 200 side by side. I read both, in full, in T1 PDFs.**

| | Article 200, first proviso | GNCTD Act s.24, first proviso |
|---|---|---|
| on a **re-passed** bill | *"the Governor **shall not withhold assent therefrom**"* | *"the Lieutenant Governor **shall declare either that he assents to the Bill or that he reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President**"* |

**In Delhi, second-round reservation for the President is expressly authorised by the governing
statute.** The very act the Supreme Court held illegal for the Governor of Tamil Nadu in April 2025
(conclusion VII) is, for the Delhi LG, written into section 24 in terms. Any series or ledger record
built on "second-round reservation" must therefore treat Delhi as a different regime, not as a
state. Section 24's second proviso also has **four** mandatory-reservation triggers (a)–(d) where
Article 200's second proviso has one.

Section 25 of the GNCTD Act mirrors Article 201 exactly, including the **six-month** obligation on
the Assembly and the silence on the President.

#### 4.5 Carried from part 01: Delhi and Puducherry vote in the GST Council

Part 01 hands across that **Delhi and Puducherry vote in the GST Council by force of Article
366(26B)** as inserted by the Constitution (101st Amendment) Act 2016, which makes a Union Territory
with a Legislature a "State" for Article 279A. **Set that against §4.3 and it is the sharpest single
statement of the anomaly:** the same Delhi government whose statutory definition of "Government" was
converted into "the Lieutenant Governor" by section 21(3) in April 2021, and whose control of its
own officers was made subject to the LG's final say from May 2023, **casts a state's vote in the GST
Council.** It is a "State" for the purpose of a body the Union chairs and a Union territory for the
purpose of running itself. I have not found any instrument that reconciles the two; I do not assert
that none exists.

**Also carried, from part 04:** the UT-with-Legislature block — Delhi, Puducherry and Jammu &
Kashmir — is **₹60,007.07 crore of Actual FY2024-25 Union transfers and ₹76,999.80 crore at BE
2026-27**, and is the whole of the ₹77,000 crore difference between the two "total transfers to
States" figures printed in one Union Budget. **These three are inside the Union's transfers total
and are governed through an LG.** Part 04 owns that quantity; this part records only that the

### §5 — Puducherry: the Administrator, not the Lieutenant Governor

**Instrument retrieved (T1).** *The Government of Union Territories Act, 1963 (ACT NO. 20 OF 1963)
[As on the **6th April, 2026**]*, **37 pages**, from
`https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/1546/1/A1963-20%20%282%29.pdf` (same `--resolve`
treatment; note the first bitstream path I tried, `a1963-20.pdf`, 302-redirected to HTML — the
correct path carries a `(2)` suffix, `A1963-20%20%282%29.pdf`).

**Point of law that matters for how this instrument is populated.** Puducherry is **not** under
Article 239AA. It is under **Article 239A** and the Government of Union Territories Act 1963, and
the statute calls the office the **"Administrator"** throughout, never "Lieutenant Governor". The
designation as Lieutenant Governor is by Presidential notification under Article 239(1), not by the
Act. **Any series keyed on "Lieutenant Governor" will silently mix two legal offices — the Delhi LG
under Article 239AA/GNCTD Act, and the Puducherry Administrator under Article 239A/GUT Act 1963 — and
they do not have the same powers.** Record this as a definitional break.

**Section 44(1)**, verbatim: a Council of Ministers *"to aid and advise the Administrator … except in
so far as he is **required by or under this Act to act in his discretion**"*, with the same
refer-to-the-President proviso as Article 239AA(4). **Section 44(3): *"If and in so far as any special
responsibility of the Administrator is involved under this Act, he shall, in the exercise of his
functions, **act in his discretion**."*** **44(4): whether a matter is one for his discretion —
*"the decision of the Administrator thereon shall be final."*** **44(6): *"The question whether any,
and if so what, advice was tendered by Ministers to the Administrator **shall not be inquired into in
any court**."*** Section 45(1)–(2): the Chief Minister and Ministers are **appointed by the
President** and *"shall hold office during the pleasure of the President."*

**Section 25 (Assent to Bills)** replicates Article 200 with the **same Delhi-style divergence in the
first proviso**: on a re-passed Bill *"the Administrator shall declare either that he assents to the
Bill **or that he reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President**"*. Its second proviso
has **four** mandatory-reservation triggers (a)–(d), including any Bill relating to *"entry 1 or
entry 2 of the State List"*. **Section 51** is the failure-of-machinery provision, with the same
two limbs as Article 239AB, including the wide *"for the proper administration of the Union
territory it is necessary or expedient so to do."*

**So, stated cleanly: on the very question the Supreme Court decided against the Governor of Tamil
Nadu in April 2025 — second-round reservation for the President — both Union Territories with
Legislatures have express statutory authority that a State's Governor does not.** That is a
legislative fact, not an interpretive one, and it favours the Union's position in both territories.

**The Puducherry litigation.** *K. Lakshminarayanan* — a Madras High Court single-judge judgment of
**30 April 2019** holding that the Administrator/LG must act on the aid and advice of the Council of
Ministers and may not interfere in day-to-day administration, later carried to a Division Bench and
to the Supreme Court. **I did not retrieve any judgment in this line, at any level, from an official
court host.** I therefore state only that the litigation exists and that I have not established its
outcome. See §Sources NOT retrieved. **Do not author a Puducherry litigation record on this part's
evidence.**

**Puducherry in the Presidential Reference.** What *is* established at T1: in Special Reference No.
1 of 2025, *"Mr. Vinay Navare for the **Union Territory of Puducherry**"* appeared among the counsel
listed at paragraph 3, page 6 — that is, on the side arguing **for** the reference and **against**

**And, from part 01:** Puducherry votes in the GST Council by force of Article 366(26B). The same
observation as for Delhi in §4.5 applies with equal force.

November 2025 opinion.** Neither side disputes the loop; they dispute whether a court may open it.

### §7 — Who appoints, who removes, on whose advice: Articles 153–159

**All verbatim from the Constitution PDF, pages 100–101, printed pages 70–71. Read directly.**

- **153.** *"There shall be a Governor for each State: Provided that nothing in this article shall
  prevent the appointment of the **same person as Governor for two or more States**."*
- **154(1).** *"The executive power of the State shall be **vested in the Governor**."*
- **155. Appointment of Governor.** *"The Governor of a State shall be **appointed by the President
  by warrant under his hand and seal**."* — **twenty-two words, and they are the whole of it.** No
  consultation with the State. No consultation with the Chief Minister. No eligibility criterion
  beyond Article 157. No procedure.
- **156. Term of office.** *"(1) The Governor shall **hold office during the pleasure of the
  President**. (2) The Governor may, by writing under his hand addressed to the President, resign
  his office. (3) **Subject to the foregoing provisions of this article**, a Governor shall hold
  office for a term of five years …"*
- **157.** The only qualifications: **citizen of India**, **aged thirty-five**.
- **158.** Not a member of a House; no other office of profit; **158(3A)**, inserted by the Seventh
  Amendment 1956, apportions emoluments where one person is Governor of several States.
- **159.** The oath — *"preserve, protect and defend the Constitution and the law and … devote
  myself to the **service and well-being of the people of** ……(name of the State)"* — sworn before
  the Chief Justice of the High Court **of the State**, not before any Union authority. The April
  2025 judgment leaned on this at paragraph 437; the November 2025 opinion did not address it.

**Read 156(1) against 156(3) and the asymmetry is exactly the one in Articles 200/201.** A five-year
term is stated, and then made *"subject to"* pleasure. The number that looks like a protection is
subordinated to the discretion that removes it. **In both cases the Constitution states a period and
then withholds its force from the person the period was supposed to protect.** That parallel between
Article 156 and Article 200 is, I think, the correct frame for this whole part: **the office is
constituted so that the Union's appointee is bound by no clock and the State is bound by every
clock.**

**Note also Article 152, page 100:** *"In this Part, unless the context otherwise requires, the
expression 'State' does not include the State of Jammu and Kashmir."* This footnote-level text
survives in the 1 May 2024 edition. **It touches Jammu and Kashmir and is therefore not developed
here** — see §Boundary against phases 11 and 12.

#### 8.4 The MHA Memorandum of 4 February 2016 — **A-3 IS CORRECTED: it IS published, inside the judgment, in full**

**I re-verified this myself in the Tamil Nadu PDF I hold, and my earlier §A-3 classification was
wrong.** 2025 INSC 481 reproduces **two** office memoranda of 4 February 2016 **in full** — at
**paragraph 385, pages 351–354** (addressed to Ministries/Departments of the Government of India) and
**paragraph 387, pages 355–357** (addressed to all States/UTs). Both bear **File No.
23/18/2015-Judl & PP (Part III)**, Ministry of Home Affairs, Judicial & PP Section, NDCG-II Building,
4th Floor, Jai Singh Road, New Delhi-110001, signed **Thangkholun Haokip, Under Secretary**.
I located these by grepping the judgment text for the file number and read both.

**From the first memorandum, verbatim (para 385, judgment pages 351–352):**
> *"The undersigned is directed to invite to this Ministry's **D.O. letter no: 23/33/1992-Judl dated
> 22.09.1992** duly conveying the guidelines formulated and approved by **the Cabinet** for disposal
> of State legislative proposals. **Despite these clear guidelines, it is observed that undue delay
> is caused in taking a final decision on such Bills.** … ii). **A time limit of maximum 3 months be
> strictly adhered to** for finalizing the Bills after their receipt from the State Governments."*

**From the second memorandum, verbatim (para 387, judgment pages 355–356):**
> *"the Ministry of Home Affairs is **the nodal Ministry** for processing and conveying a final
> decision with respect to State Legislations under Article 201 read with Article 254(2) … Recently,
> the matter has been reviewed and **a timeline of maximum three months has been fixed for disposing
> State Bills** … from the date of their receipt from the State Government. A maximum period of one
> month has been kept for Inter-Ministerial consultation and next one month has been kept for
> obtaining the comments/clarifications of the State Government … **If the requisite
> clarifications/views are not received from the State Government within the stipulated one month's
> time, it will be construed that the State Government agrees with the observation and has no
> comments to offer.**"*

**This is the most important single document in this part after the two 2025 judgments, and it cuts
against the Union.** In 2016 the Union's own nodal ministry, on Cabinet-approved guidelines of 1992,
**fixed a three-month outer limit on Article 201 disposal because it found *"undue delay"* in its own
system.** In 2025 the Union argued to the Supreme Court that no time limit may be imposed, and the
Constitution Bench agreed (165.7), holding at para 113 that *"the Ministry's circulars cannot be
incorporated by way of judicial interpretation … as a constitutional fetter."*
**The two positions are not contradictory in law** — an internal administrative timeline is not a
constitutional obligation, and the Union may consistently hold both. **But they are the same
institution stating that three months is the right period and that three months may not be
required.** That is a fact about the record, not a characterisation.
**Note also the asymmetry inside the memorandum itself:** where the *State* fails to reply within one
month, its silence *"will be construed"* as agreement. There is no equivalent construction against
the Union.

**Corrected classification of A-3:** the memoranda are **published — but only because litigation put
them on a judicial record in 2025**, nine years after issue. **I found no independent publication of
either on any government site.** The correct label is therefore **not `withheld` and not
`not-published` simpliciter, but `published-only-through-litigation`**, which is a distinct and worse
condition: the document's availability is contingent on someone suing. The **prior instrument it
depends on — MHA D.O. letter No. 23/33/1992-Judl dated 22.09.1992, conveying *Cabinet-approved*
guidelines — remains entirely unretrieved and is a live absence.**

## Candidate ledger records

**LR-1 — The Constitution states a deadline for the elected legislature and none for the Union's
appointee, and both sides argue from that same silence.**
*What happened.* Article 200 gives the Governor three options and the words *"as soon as possible"*
attached only to returning a Bill. Article 201 gives the President two options and no period at all.
The single fixed period in the whole scheme — **six months** — binds the **State legislature** to
reconsider a returned Bill (proviso to Article 201). No deeming provision, no lapse, no default.
*Objective stated at announcement.* None stated in the text; the constitutional-design case is put by
the Union in the Presidential Reference recitals of 13 May 2025 — *"polycentric considerations, inter
alia being federalism, uniformity of laws, integrity and security of the nation, doctrine of
separation of powers"* (T1, 2025 INSC 1333 pages 2–3).
*The strongest case for the Union's reading.* The framers wrote deeming clauses into Articles 107,
108, 109(4), 109(5), 196, 197, 198(4), 198(5), 213 and 239B and **did not write one into 200 or
201** — a five-judge Bench found this decisive (2025 INSC 1333 para 117). The office is a
constitutional check, not a postbox; the second proviso makes reservation *mandatory* in a defined
case, which shows the framers contemplated the Governor stopping a Bill. And elasticity is deliberate:
*"The text of Articles 200 and 201, has been framed in such a manner, so as to provide a sense of
elasticity"* (para 115).
*The strongest case for the States' reading.* *"shall declare"* admits of no inaction; a power with no
stated period must be exercised in reasonable time; and the concrete consequence of the contrary
reading is that an unelected appointee *"would be in a position to virtually veto the functioning of
the legislative domain by a duly elected legislature by simply declaring that assent is withheld
without any further recourse"* (2023 INSC 1017 para 25). The measured facts support the concern:
bills sat 1,400 days in Tamil Nadu, 7–23 months in Kerala.
***Same facts or different facts?* SAME FACTS.** Every fact in the preceding two paragraphs is
agreed. Nobody disputes that Article 200 has no time limit; nobody disputes that ten Tamil Nadu bills
sat for over a year. **The two sides differ on the weight to give the framers' silence, not on what
the framers wrote or on what the Governors did.** That is the single most important structural
statement in this part, and it distinguishes this dispute from part 03's cess dispute, where the
parties genuinely disagree about a subtraction.

**LR-2 — The April 2025 judgment: the first deemed assent in Indian constitutional history, and its
repudiation seven months later.**
*What happened.* 8 April 2025: a two-judge Bench declared ten Tamil Nadu bills deemed assented under
Article 142 as of 18 November 2023, prescribed one-month and three-month limits on the Governor and
three months on the President, and held the Governor bound by ministerial advice. 13 May 2025: the
President referred fourteen questions under Article 143. 22 July 2025: placed before a Constitution
Bench. 19 August 2025: hearing begins. **20 November 2025: a five-judge Bench holds that timelines
cannot be judicially prescribed (165.5, 165.7), that the Governor is not bound by ministerial advice
under Article 200 (165.2), that his function is not justiciable on the merits (165.3), and that
"the Constitution, specifically Article 142 even, does not allow for the concept of 'deemed assent'
of Bills" (165.10).**
*What survives on the States' side.* A **limited mandamus** where inaction is *"prolonged,
unexplained, and indefinite"* (165.3), and the holding that Article 361 immunises the **person** but
not the **office** (165.4).
*What survives on the Tamil Nadu bills.* The relief was **not vacated** — paragraph 26: *"The
questions do not require this Court to vacate, amend, or modify the final relief granted by this
Court in State of Tamil Nadu (supra)."* **The ten bills stay assented; the doctrine that assented
them is gone.**
*Both sides in their own terms.* The Union's case is that a court may not manufacture a
constitutional consequence the text withholds, and that an Article 143 reference clarifying the law
for all States is precisely what advisory jurisdiction is for. The States' case, put to the Court and
rejected, is that the reference was *"an appeal, or review in disguise"* and *"a malafide attempt by
the Central Government to sidestep the decision of this Court"* (2025 INSC 1333 para 6). **The Court
recorded the States' allegation and dismissed it at the threshold (para 28). Both positions are on
one record.**

**LR-3 — Delhi: a Constitution Bench holding reversed by statute in eight days, under an express
constitutional licence to do so.**
*What happened.* 11 May 2023: Constitution Bench holds NCTD has legislative and executive power over
Services and the LG is bound by the Council of Ministers on them (2023 judgment, para 164(i), 162).
**19 May 2023**: Part IV-A of the GNCTD Act takes effect, creating the National Capital Civil Service
Authority, making its subject-matter one on which the LG acts *"in his sole discretion"* (s.41(1)(iii))
and providing that on difference of opinion *"the decision of the Lieutenant Governor shall be
final"* (s.45H(3)). Carried into permanent law by **Act 19 of 2023**.
*Objective stated at announcement.* The Part's own heading: *"PROVISIONS RELATING TO THE MAINTENANCE
OF THE **DEMOCRATIC AND ADMINISTRATIVE BALANCE** IN THE GOVERNANCE OF NATIONAL CAPITAL TERRITORY OF
DELHI"* (T1, consolidated Act page 20). That is the only objective statement I retrieved.
*The strongest case for the Union.* **Article 239AA(7)(b) expressly authorises it**: a law under
239AA(7)(a) *"shall not be deemed to be an amendment of this Constitution for the purposes of article
368 notwithstanding that it contains any provision which amends or has the effect of amending, this
Constitution."* Delhi is the national capital; the Union's stake in the administration of the seat of
government is not a pretext. The 2023 Bench itself said Delhi is *sui generis* — a category the Union
can legislate for.
*The strongest case for Delhi.* The 2023 Bench held the executive power of NCTD *"co-extensive with
its legislative power"*; a three-member Authority chaired by the Chief Minister but including a Chief
Secretary **"appointed by the Central Government"** (s.45A(d)) and decided by majority (s.45E(3))
can outvote the elected head 2–1 on his own officers, and even its recommendation is subject to the
LG's final say. And **Delhi votes as a State in the GST Council** (part 01).
***Same facts or different facts?* SAME FACTS, and unusually cleanly.** Both sides accept the text of
239AA(7)(b), the dates, and the composition of the Authority. The disagreement is entirely about
whether an express constitutional licence to legislate around a judgment should be used the week
after one.

**LR-4 — The chancellor loop.** The Governor is Chancellor under State statutes; removing him
requires a State Bill; that Bill needs his assent under Article 200. On the November 2025 opinion he
has discretion over it (165.2), no judicially enforceable clock (165.5), and no merits review
(165.3). **Twelve of twelve Tamil Nadu bills, five of the named Kerala bills and two of four Punjab
bills in these disputes were university bills.** Neither side disputes the loop exists. West Bengal
shows the same conflict displaced into appointments, where the Supreme Court eventually used **Article
142** to constitute the selection machinery itself (2024 INSC 469 para 10) — **the same power the
November 2025 opinion says cannot substitute for the Governor's assent.**

**LR-5 — Two Union Territories with Legislatures have a statutory power their State counterparts do
not, on the exact point that was litigated.** GNCTD Act s.24 and Government of Union Territories Act
1963 s.25 both provide that on a **re-passed** Bill the LG/Administrator *"shall declare either that
he assents to the Bill **or that he reserves the Bill for the consideration of the President**"*,
where Article 200's first proviso says the Governor *"shall not withhold assent therefrom"*. Both
also carry **four** mandatory-reservation triggers against Article 200's one. **Retrieved from two
T1 consolidated Acts.** Any cross-territory count of "second-round reservations" is comparing a
statutory power with an alleged illegality.

**LR-6 — Three commissions recommended consulting the Chief Minister on the appointment and removal
of Governors, and time limits on assent. None was implemented. The Union's stated reason is that the
process is unfinished, and it has been unfinished for sixteen years.**
*What was recommended.* **NCRWC (2002), read at T1**: appointment *"after consultation with the Chief
Minister of that State"*; removal or transfer *"by following a similar procedure as for appointment"*;
the Sarkaria 4.16.01 criteria (eminent, from outside the State, detached from local politics, not
recently active in politics); and **six months for the Governor and three months for the President**
(para 8.14.4). **Sarkaria (1988)**, through official quoting instruments only: the Governor *"must
abide by the advice of his Council of Ministers"* under Article 200 save in *"rare and exceptional"*
patent-unconstitutionality cases (para 5.6.13, quoted at 2025 INSC 481 para 103). **Punchhi (2010)**,
through the Supreme Court only: *"concrete timelines are sine qua non"*, and specifically **six
months in Article 201 for the President** (2025 INSC 481 paras 137–140).
*What happened.* Punchhi's 273 recommendations went to the **11th Inter-State Council meeting, 16
July 2016**, which referred them to the Standing Committee; the Standing Committee met **9 April
2017, 25 November 2017 and 25 May 2018**; and then, per MHA Annual Report 2024-25 para 17.7,
*"**It has been decided to obtain updated comments of the State Governments/UT Administrations on the
recommendations of the Standing Committee. In view of this, a meeting of the ISC to discuss the
Punchhi Commission recommendations could not be held.**"*
*Objective stated at announcement.* None; the Annual Report states a status, not an objective.
*The strongest case for the Union.* Article 155 confers the appointment power on the President
without qualification, and adding a consultation requirement is a **constitutional amendment**, not
an administrative decision — Punchhi itself *"advocated for the immediate implementation … by way of
a Constitutional Amendment"* (2025 INSC 481 para 142). The Inter-State Council is a consultative body
of the States themselves, and it was **the Chief Ministers** who asked in 2016 that the Standing
Committee examine the recommendations first (AR 2016-17 para 4.7). **The delay is, on the Union's own
record, a delay the States asked for.**
*The strongest case for the States.* The forum has met **eleven times in thirty-six years and not
once in the last ten**. A body that does not meet cannot decide, and a recommendation referred to a
committee of a body that does not meet is not under consideration in any meaningful sense.
***Same facts or different facts?* SAME FACTS.** Both sides would accept every date above. **This is
the third quantity in this part where the disagreement is entirely about the weight of an agreed
record.**

**LR-7 — Article 356 since 2014: four proclamations outside Jammu and Kashmir, one very long, and no
official list of any of them.**
*What happened.* Arunachal Pradesh (Jan–Feb 2016, ~24 days), **Uttarakhand (27 March – 11 May 2016,
~45 days)**, **Maharashtra (12–23 November 2019, ~11 days)**, Manipur (13 February 2025 – 4 February
2026, **~357 days**). Puducherry 2021 belongs to **section 51 of the Government of Union Territories
Act 1963**, not Article 356. J&K is excluded by phase boundary.
*Instrument.* For Uttarakhand and Maharashtra, the **Rajya Sabha laying record under Article 356(3)**
— which names the Proclamation's G.S.R. number, the accompanying Order, and separately lays *"the
Report of the Governor … recommending the issue of the Proclamation"*. **That laying record is a
genuine one-ledger instrument: the Union's proclamation and the Governor's report are placed before
the House together.** For Arunachal 2016 and Manipur 2025 there is no such record in this part.
*The absence.* **The Ministry of Home Affairs — the nodal ministry, which describes *"imposition of
President's Rule"* in its own Annual Report as part of the Centre-State Division's mandate — has
never published a list of Article 356 proclamations in any of the four Annual Reports checked, and
its Centre-State Division page carries none.** The last official cumulative figure of any kind is
**NCRWC's 111 invocations as at 2002**, and that figure mixes Article 356 with section 51 of the GUT
Act.
*Strongest case each way.* The Union's: each proclamation is laid before both Houses within two
months under Article 356(3) and must be approved by resolution, so Parliament is the register and a
ministry list would be duplicative. The States': a laying record is a scatter of individual entries
across decades of debates, findable only if you already know the date — **which is precisely what a
series is for, and precisely what is missing.**

name. `main.sci.gov.in` and `digiscr.sci.gov.in` **do not resolve on 1.1.1.1 at all** (see §Sources
NOT retrieved). `indiacode.nic.in` is alive via Akamai at 94.202.207.51/.59 and served both
consolidated Acts.

**PR-2 — "Reserved for the President" is at least three different acts, and the same word is used
for all three.** (i) A reservation the Constitution **mandates** (Article 200 second proviso; and in
Delhi/Puducherry, four statutory triggers). (ii) A reservation of a bill **presented for the first
time**, which the November 2025 opinion holds is within the Governor's discretion. (iii) A
reservation in the **second round**, after a bill has been returned and re-passed, which the April
2025 judgment held illegal for a Governor (conclusion VII) but which **statute expressly authorises
for the Delhi LG and the Puducherry Administrator**. **Tamil Nadu's twelve-row table contains types
(ii) and (iii) in the same column.** Any count that does not separate them is meaningless.

**PR-3 — The pendency of a bill becomes measurable only at the moment it stops being pending, and
in each retrieved case that moment was caused by the litigation.** Punjab: two Money Bills
recommended *"after the institution of the Petition"* (2023 INSC 1017 para 10). Kerala: all eight
bills disposed of on **28 November 2023**, eight days after notice, and the Court's own finding is
*"It is only after the Court had issued notice … that the Governor has acted"* (para 11). Tamil Nadu:
all twelve bills acted on **13 November 2023**, three days after *State of Punjab*. **The observation
instrument is the writ petition. This is a selection effect on the entire evidence base and it is
not correctable from within the record.**

**PR-4 — The MHA has written instructions on time-limits for bills reserved for the President, and
they are known to this instrument only through two judgments.** The April 2025 judgment cites a
*"Memorandum dated 04.02.2016 issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs"* (conclusion XIX) and
*"Circulars issued by the Ministry of Home Affairs"* (Reference opinion para 113, citing TN paras
404–408 and 417). Neither Bench reproduces the text. **A Union executive instruction that both a
two-judge Bench and a Constitution Bench treat as real, whose content the public record does not
carry.** See §Absences A-3.

**PR-5 — Two counts of the same Tamil Nadu dispute: ten and twelve.** The petition, the operative
order and every conclusion speak of **ten** bills. The Court's own factual table has **twelve** rows.
The difference is Bills 25/2022 and 26/2022, reserved for the President in the **first** round on
13.11.2023 and therefore never re-enacted, and whose presidential disposition the table records as
**"Status not on record"**. **Both numbers are correct for different questions, and both are in the
same judgment.** Any record must state which.

**PR-7 — Two MHA Annual Reports give two dates for the submission of the Punchhi Commission report.**
AR 2016-17 para 4.8: *"submitted its report … on **30.03.2010**"*. AR 2024-25 para 17.7: *"submitted
its report to the Government on **31st March, 2010**"*. **Same ministry, same series, one day
apart.** Trivial in itself; it matters because it is a **same-instrument** disagreement, so no more
independent source can settle it, and because it shows the Annual Report is not internally reconciled
across years.

**PR-8 — Punchhi's reproduction of NCRWC's Governor time-limit, carried into a Supreme Court
judgment, misstates NCRWC.** 2025 INSC 481 para 141, reciting Punchhi reciting NCRWC: *"a time-limit
- say **a period of four months** - within which the Governor should take a decision"*. **The NCRWC
report's own text at para 8.14.4, read directly, says **six months**.** The three-month figure for the
President is consistent across both. **A number degraded in transmission through two official
documents and then printed by the Supreme Court. Flagged, not reconciled.**

**PR-9 — "Article 356 and analogous provisions" is a composite, and every historical count uses it.**
NCRWC's **111** invocations expressly bundles **13** applications of **section 51 of the Government
of Union Territories Act 1963** with Article 356 proper, and further subtracts **10** applied
*"technically due to the mechanics of the Constitution"*. **No published figure separates the
statutes.** Any modern count that adds Puducherry 2021 to Uttarakhand 2016 is doing the same
bundling without saying so.

**PR-10 — The NCRWC report is readable only through the Internet Archive; the live official host
serves an empty shell.** `legalaffairs.gov.in/ncrwc-report/` returns HTTP 200 with
`__NEXT_DATA__` = `{"pageProps":{}}`. **A 200 is not a retrieval.** Anyone sweeping for the NCRWC
report by status code would record it present and come away with nothing. This is the same failure
shape as M3's silent-drop: the transport succeeded and the content did not arrive.

**PR-6 — "Lieutenant Governor" is not one office.** Delhi's LG holds under Article 239AA and the
GNCTD Act 1991. Puducherry's officer is the **"Administrator"** in the Government of Union
Territories Act 1963 throughout, holding under Article 239A, designated Lieutenant Governor by
notification under Article 239(1). Their statutory powers differ (compare GNCTD s.41 as amended in
2023 with GUT Act s.44(3)–(6)). **A series keyed on "LG" merges them.**

## Absences

*As the brief predicted, this is the largest section.*

**A-1 — There is no national count of bills pending with Governors, and the quantity at the centre
of a constitutional dispute decided twice by the Supreme Court in 2025 appears to be collected by
nobody.**
**Classification: see §8 — this is the finding that turns on the sweep, and the sweep's positive
control governs whether it is `not-collected` or `unestablished`.**
What is certain from what I read: **neither the April 2025 judgment nor the November 2025
Constitution Bench opinion cites any aggregate figure for bills pending with Governors.** Both are
about the constitutional consequence of pendency. **A Constitution Bench decided the legal effect of
a quantity without either party placing a national figure for that quantity before it.** That is a
fact about two 2025 Supreme Court records that I established by reading them, and it stands whatever
§8 concludes about the parliamentary corpus.
**What the Court instead has is bill-by-bill, state-by-state, only for litigated states, only at the
moment of litigation.** Tamil Nadu 12 rows (2025 INSC 481), Kerala 8 named bills (order 29.11.2023),
Punjab 4 named bills + 3 Money Bills (2023 INSC 1017). **Nothing for the other twenty-plus states.**
**Why the absence is structural and not incidental.** There is no reporting obligation anywhere in
Articles 200 or 201. A Governor need not notify anyone that a Bill is with him — the Punjab judgment
records at paragraph 8 that *"the Governor did not 'declare' in any public notification that he is
withholding his assent to the Bills."* If the deciding officer need not announce the decision, no
register follows from the Constitution, and none is created by it.

**A-2 — The single aggregate figure that does exist is a party's, unverified, undated and undefined.**
*"The Governor has, as a matter of fact, assented to as many as **185 Bills**"* — senior counsel for
the Principal Secretary to the Governor of Punjab, recorded at 2023 INSC 1017 para 13(iii) and again
at para 27. **No period. No denominator. No source. The Court held it irrelevant rather than
verifying it.** This is exactly the trap in §(c) of my brief and it is the strongest illustration of
it I found: it is on the most authoritative record available in India and it is still the Union
appointee's number.

**A-3 — `published-only-through-litigation`: the MHA memoranda of 4 February 2016, and the 1992
Cabinet guidelines behind them, which remain unretrieved.**
**CORRECTED FINDING.** My first pass classified the 4 February 2016 memorandum `not-published`.
**That was wrong and I found it wrong by grepping my own copy of 2025 INSC 481 for the file number.**
The Supreme Court reproduces **two** memoranda of that date **in full** at paragraphs **385** (pages
351–354) and **387** (pages 355–357), both File No. **23/18/2015-Judl & PP (Part III)**. **The text is
public — but only because the State of Tamil Nadu sued and the Court chose to print it.** No
independent government publication of either memorandum was found. See §8.4 for the verbatim text
and why it cuts against the Union.
**What remains a clean absence** is the instrument the 2016 memorandum itself relies on: **MHA D.O.
letter No. 23/33/1992-Judl dated 22.09.1992, *"conveying the guidelines formulated and approved by
the Cabinet for disposal of State legislative proposals"*.** Cabinet-approved guidelines on how the
Union handles State Bills, cited by name in a document a Constitution Bench discussed, **entirely
unretrieved**. Classification `not-published` — I have no named requester, no specific request and no
date of refusal, so `withheld` is not available.

**A-3a — The reports of both Commissions on Centre–State Relations are currently unobtainable.**

**A-3b — No Article 356 Gazette notification was read, for any proclamation.** Uttarakhand 2016 and

**A-5 — No official disposition register for Article 201 references exists in anything I retrieved.**
The Tamil Nadu table shows the President withholding assent on 15, 16, 18 and 26 February and 18
March 2024, granting assent once on 18 February 2024, and **two bills marked *"Assent is awaited

Supreme Court judgment, about the President's action on a State Bill — is itself the finding: **even
the Court could not establish what had happened to two of the twelve.**

**A-6 — Reasons for withholding assent are not published anywhere.** Article 200 requires none.
Article 201 requires none. The 2025 Constitution Bench held merits are **not justiciable** (165.3,
165.6) and that Article 361 immunises the person (165.4). **The result is a decision that need not be
announced, need not be reasoned, need not be timed and cannot be reviewed on its merits — with the

that sentence is from the Court's own opinion.

## Disciplining measure

**The question the phase asked me: is a Supreme Court judgment an instrument that puts both sides'
facts on ONE ledger, and does it do what part 03 found the CAG does — perform a subtraction neither
party will perform, producing a fact neither party supplied?**

**My answer, per quantity, is that a judgment is a genuinely stronger instrument than part 01's GST
Council minute book, and genuinely weaker than part 03's CAG audit, and the reason is precise: it
independently *finds* facts but does not independently *generate* them.**

| quantity | is there a one-ledger instrument? | does it produce a fact neither party supplied? | verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tamil Nadu bill-by-bill dates (12 rows × 4 dates)** | **Yes, and it is the strongest in this phase.** 2025 INSC 481 pp.13–17 puts the State's pleading and the Governor's own letters of 28.11.2023 and 04.12.2023 on one record, and the table is the Court's compilation of both | **Partly.** The dates are documentary and neither party contested them. **The Court's own contribution is the juxtaposition**: acting on all twelve bills on 13.11.2023, three days after *State of Punjab*, is a fact that emerges only from putting the two sides' dates side by side — which is what a subtraction is | **Strong.** A judgment does perform this operation, on documents |
| **Kerala's "no reason or justification has been adduced"** (order 29.11.2023 para 10) | **Yes.** The Attorney General placed the Additional Chief Secretary's own communication on the record | **YES, and this is the clearest instance in the part.** *"No reason or justification has been adduced before this Court"* is a **finding about the absence of an explanation**, made by a third party after hearing both. Neither party supplied it — the State could only allege it, the Governor's side could only fail to rebut it. **This is the judicial analogue of the CAG's subtraction** | **Passes the independence test** |
| **Kerala's "only after the Court had issued notice"** (para 11) | Yes | **YES.** It is a causal finding the Court could make and neither party could | **Passes** |
| **Punjab's "185 Bills"** | Yes — it is on the record | **NO.** Counsel supplied it; the Court neither verified nor rejected it, only held it irrelevant | **Fails. This is part 01's shape exactly**: a shared ledger of *arguments*. It must never be authored as a data point |
| **West Bengal's 35 / 24 / 27 / 28 / 15 / 7 / 8** | Yes — 2024 INSC 469 records the State's proposals and the Chancellor's responses on one document | **Partly.** The counts come from the parties; *"approximately [35]"* is the Court's own approximation, flagged as such. But para 4's diagnosis — *"The rift … is the root cause of stalemate"* — is a finding neither party would state | **Mixed** |
| **A national count of bills pending with Governors** | **NO INSTRUMENT AT ALL.** No judgment, no ministry report, no register | n/a | **This quantity has no disciplining measure because it has no measure** |
| **The Union's reasons for Governors' conduct** | Formally yes (pleadings), but 2025 INSC 1333 at 165.3/165.6 holds the merits **not justiciable**, and 165.4 holds Article 361 bars subjecting the Governor personally to proceedings | **NO, and now cannot.** The Court has held it may not conduct a merits review | **Closed off going forward** |

**The three things that follow, and they complete the phase's finding.**

**(1) A judgment is a stronger disciplining measure than a negotiating minute, and the difference is
that a judge is obliged to decide.** Part 01 found the GST Council minute book to be a shared ledger
of *arguments* because every number originates with a party and nobody adjudicates between them. A
judgment has the same input problem — every number still originates with a party — **but it adds an
output that no minute book has: a third party states which facts it found, in terms, and is
answerable for the statement.** *"No reason or justification has been adduced"* and *"it is only
after the Court had issued notice … that the Governor has acted"* are not either party's assertions.
They are new propositions about the world, produced by the instrument.

**(2) But a judgment is weaker than the CAG's cess-to-reserve-fund audit, and the difference is
arithmetic versus adjudication.** Part 03 found the CAG passes because the auditor **performs a
subtraction the payer will not perform** — the auditor computes a number that did not previously
exist in anyone's books. A court does not compute. **A court chooses between, and reasons about,
numbers that both parties brought.** Where the parties bring no number, the court has none: this is
why the "185 Bills" figure survives unchecked and why no Bench in either 2025 judgment could state a
national pendency figure. **The CAG's independence is generative; the Court's is only evaluative.**
That is the split the phase was looking for, stated for this subject.

**(3) The systematic bias, which is a finding in its own right.** **One-ledger evidence exists for
Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Punjab, Telangana, Delhi and West Bengal — and for no other state.** It exists
because those states sued. It exists **only from the moment they sued**, and PR-3 shows the suing is
what ended the pendency being measured. So:
- **The states with the worst relationship with their Governor are the only states with a public
  record of it.** A state whose Governor sits on bills and which does not litigate leaves no trace
  at all.
- **The evidence base is therefore biased in a direction that cannot be signed.** It over-samples
  conflict (only disputes generate records) *and* under-states duration (the clock stops when the
  writ is filed). These pull opposite ways and no retrieved instrument lets me net them.
- **Nothing in the unlitigated states is knowable from any instrument I found.** That is not a gap in
  my sweep; it is a property of the instrument, and it is the reason A-1 matters more than any number
  in this report.

**Caveat, as the brief requires and as the evidence bears out.** A judgment supplies **findings of
fact on the matters pleaded**, not a measurement series. The Tamil Nadu table is a table because
Tamil Nadu pleaded twelve bills; it has no rows for the thirteenth. The Kerala range is a range
because that is what was pleaded. **Every "series" in §Candidate series above is an event, not an
observation on a calendar, and I have marked each one accordingly.**

## Boundary against phases 11 and 12

**Jammu and Kashmir is bounded away from this part entirely and nothing here re-authors L-0110,
L-0118, L-0123, L-0125, L-0127, L-0128, L-0138, L-0141, L-0143, L-0146 or L-0147.**

Explicitly:

1. **No Article 356 proclamation, Governor's rule or President's rule in Jammu and Kashmir is
   recorded, counted or dated in this part.** The Article 356 material at §8 and the candidate series
   `art356-proclamations` are scoped **"excluding Jammu and Kashmir"** in their own unit definition,
   not merely in commentary. **A downstream reader must not read this part's count as a national
   count**; it is a national count minus J&K by construction, and it is therefore **not comparable
   with any published national total**. That non-comparability is itself the boundary working.
2. **The Lieutenant Governor of Jammu and Kashmir is not this part's subject.** §4 is Delhi under
   Article 239AA; §5 is Puducherry under Article 239A and the Government of Union Territories Act
   1963. **Neither analysis extends to J&K**, whose LG holds under the Jammu and Kashmir
   Reorganisation Act 2019 — a different statute this part did not retrieve and does not discuss.
3. **Article 152's words *"the expression 'State' does not include the State of Jammu and Kashmir"*,
   which survive in the 1 May 2024 official text, are recorded at §7 as a textual observation with an
   explicit hand-off** and are **not** developed into a finding here.
4. **The fiscal treatment of J&K as part of the UT-with-Legislature transfers block is part 04's
   quantity**, noted at §4.5 only to record that the three LG territories move together fiscally.
   **No J&K figure is stated.**
5. **Where any subject-matter in this part touches J&K, phases 11 and 12 own it.** If a downstream
   author needs a J&K assent, Article 356 or LG record, it must come from those ledger records and
   not from part 05.
