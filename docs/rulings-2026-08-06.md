# Four operator rulings, 6 August 2026 — what was written in, and what is now owed

The governing principle, recorded as such:

> **No record or claim stands on a source that is not credibly independent of what it establishes.**

This batch wrote the principle and the four rulings into `CLAUDE.md`, the `assessment` definition in
`schemas/ledger.schema.json` and the `Assessment` doc comment in `lib/types.ts`; built the two
authorised enum values; and reported the populations each ruling governs. **No record was rescored
and no citation was re-tiered.** The whole set moves together, once.

## 1. The nine `worked` records against the independence requirement

Independence is asked of the *relationship* between a source and the claim, not of the document's
tier. Three degrees are distinguished, because collapsing them would assert more than the corpus
holds:

- **none** — every source is the announcing body or its own ministry;
- **intra-state** — a different institution of the same state measures the announcing body;
- **external** — a source outside the Indian state.

## 5. Scope only: the correction-sibling gate

**The question.** Where a record carries a correction, do its *other* prose fields still assert what
was withdrawn?

**Why it is owed.** L-0114 is the third instance and it is the one that documents the failure mode
in its own text: the summary's universal claim was corrected on 5 August, and the same universal
claim in the `caveat` survived, *because the correction pass searched for the word "ever" and that
sentence omitted it*. A correction that fixes one field and leaves the withdrawn claim standing in a
sibling has published two incompatible sentences on one page and marked the wrong one as authoritative.

**Population.** 30 fields across 24 ledger and provenance records carry a correction marker
(`CORRECTED <date>`, `previously read/said/stated`, `has been corrected`).

**The mechanical shape, and its ceiling.** A correction that *quotes the wording it withdrew* is
machine-checkable: extract the quoted string, and search every other prose field on the same record
for it — normalised through `tools/lib/page-text.mjs`, and matched on content-word overlap rather
than equality, because L-0114's surviving sentence differed from the corrected one by a single word.
**Only 6 of the 30 quote the withdrawn wording** (L-0114, L-0195 ×3 quotes, L-0011, L-0018, L-0021,
P-81). So a gate written today reaches 20 per cent of its subject.

**Therefore the gate is two things, and the authoring half comes first:**

1. **An authoring convention** — a correction states what it withdrew, in quotation marks, in the
   same field. Enforceable immediately by a check that a correction-marked field contains a quoted
   span; that check is cheap, its scope is the 30 fields above, and it would fail on 24 of them.
2. **The sibling assertion** — for each quoted withdrawn span, no other prose field on the record
   still asserts it. Runs only over what (1) produces, and grows as corrections are authored.

**What it cannot do.** It cannot find a withdrawn claim that was never written down, and it cannot
judge paraphrase — a sibling that restates the withdrawn claim in different words passes. Both
limits belong in the tool's own header rather than in a green scope line, on the standing rule that
a gate names what it does not cover.

**Not built in this batch.** Scoping only, per the sizing constraint.

## 6. What L-0218, L-0219 and L-0220 would cite instead

Under the principle, an instrument citing its own private repository as evidence for a claim about
itself fails twice over: the source is not independent of what it establishes, and a reader cannot
reach it — the cited `github.com` URLs return 404.

| Record | Cites now | Would cite instead | Residual |
|---|---|---|---|
| L-0220 | private repo history | **`docs/derivations.md`**, which prints the rule beside every number and rebuilds from `/data` | none once published — the derivation is reproducible by anyone holding the corpus |
| L-0219 | private repo reference | a derivation of the same shape for the 278 / 92 / 139 partition, generated from `/data` | **the 139 unaddressable citations** — the partition is reproducible, the *unaddressability* is a property of the live web on a date, and nothing stores that |
| L-0218 | an internal verification log; the public URL is the MEA page under test, not the log | `tools/source-response-check.mjs` and **its stored output** | **three channels** — the record reports retrieval *results* for which no retrieval was stored |

**The blocking condition is publication, not authorship.** `docs/derivations.md` exists and is
committed, but nothing on the public site links to it and the repository is not public, so citing it
today would replace one unreachable source with another. The route within the instrument's own
control is to **render the derivations at a public route** on the site, which requires no decision
about the repository. Until that happens, L-0220's replacement is written but not citable.

**What remains unreproducible after all of that:**

- **L-0219's 139 unaddressable citations.** A count of what could not be resolved on a given day is
  not a property of the corpus; re-running the check next month gives a different number. Only a
  *stored, dated sweep result* makes it reproducible, and none was kept.
- **L-0218's three channels.** The record states what was found at three MEA publication routes.
  `source-response-check.mjs` re-runs live and discards its output, so the record asserts results
  that no artefact holds. This is the same defect as L-0219 and the same fix: the sweep must write a
  dated result file into the corpus, or the record must be reworded to a claim about the tool rather
  than about the web.

Both are honest limits of a live-web claim, not of the sourcing. Naming them is the deliverable.

## 7. Draft for `/method`, for operator review

> ### What it takes for this instrument to say a policy worked
>
> Nine records out of 226 say a measure worked. That number is small, and it would be easy to read
> it as a verdict on the government. It is not. It is a verdict on the evidence, and this section
> explains the standard that produces it so you can judge whether the standard is the right one.
>
> **A body's own announcement is not evidence that the body succeeded.** This instrument now works
> to a single principle: *no record or claim stands on a source that is not credibly independent of
> what it establishes.* A ministry press release is a perfectly good source for what a ministry
> said, when it said it, and often for the underlying figures it publishes. It is not a source for
> whether that ministry achieved what it promised. Those are different claims and they need
> different evidence. Most of the records here that say a measure worked rest, in whole or in part,
> on the announcing department's own account of its own performance — so they do not meet the
> standard, and they will move.
>
> **Not meeting the standard does not mean the measure failed.** This is the part most easily got
> wrong, including by us. If nobody independent has measured whether a policy worked, the honest
> finding is that the outcome is *unestablished* — not that it went badly. Records in that position
> move to *partly* or *contested*, never to *failed*. An instrument that turned missing evidence
> into a negative verdict would be making exactly the same mistake as one that turned a press
> release into a positive one, only in the other direction.
>
> **If a promise had several parts and one was never measured, the promise is not scored as
> delivered.** Governments announce packages. A railway electrification programme promises track
> and it promises lower emissions; a banking programme promises capital and it promises stronger,
> better-run banks. Where the first part is measured and the second is not, this instrument records
> *partly*. There is no rule that lets the biggest or most visible part stand in for the whole, and
> a record explaining why one part should count for less has described a departure from the
> standard rather than earned an exception to it.
>
> **A promise with a number but no date now has its own label.** The Home Minister's commitment to
> fence the entire 1,643 kilometres of the Indo-Myanmar border is a real, quotable, countable
> promise. It also has no deadline, so it can never become overdue, and this instrument used to file
> such promises as having *no stated objective* — which was true about the deadline and plainly
> false about the promise. Those records now read *stated commitment, no deadline*. **Progress
> against them is still reported.** The label says the clock is missing, not the objective.
>
> **Foreign governments' documents now sit in their own tier.** A United States executive order or
> Federal Register notice is a primary document and a strong one, but it is not an Indian official
> source, and it is not a multilateral one either. Nineteen such citations had been filed as Indian
> official because the source ladder had nowhere else to put them. They now have a tier of their
> own — which also makes visible something the old ladder hid: a foreign government's account of an
> Indian policy *is* independent of the Indian body being assessed, in a way an Indian ministry's
> own release is not.
>
> ### Why a small "worked" count is not a finding about the government
>
> The number of records saying *worked* measures two things at once: how often policies achieved
> what was promised, and how often anyone independent of the promising body bothered to check.
> **This instrument cannot separate them, and it should not pretend to.** Where the Indian state is
> the only body that measures a thing — which is most of the time, on most of these subjects —
> raising the evidence standard necessarily lowers the count of established successes, whatever the
> policies actually did.
>
> So: read the count of *worked* records as a statement about how much of Indian policy is
> independently measured. Read it as a statement about Indian policy only where you also see the
> independent evidence cited on the record. Where a record says *partly* or *contested* because the
> outcome is unestablished, it says so in those words, and it names what would settle it. Those
> notes are the useful part. The tally is not.

**Operator decisions this draft assumes and does not make:** whether it ships as a new `/method`
section or replaces the existing sourcing paragraph; and whether the count it opens with is typed
(it must not be) or derived from `statusCounts` at build time (it must be) before publication.
